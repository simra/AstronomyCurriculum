ASTR 310 · Lecture 01

Describing Radiation: Flux, Intensity, and the Electromagnetic Spectrum

The language every later derivation in this course will use

Learning Goals

  1. Distinguish specific intensity, flux, and luminosity as distinct radiative quantities with distinct units.
  2. Derive the inverse-square dilution of flux from a point source using energy conservation across expanding spheres.
  3. Explain why intensity, unlike flux, is independent of distance along a ray in vacuum.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 5.1-5.2 (radiation, telescope light-gathering) and Chapter 18.1 (measuring the brightness of stars); this course goes beyond the introductory treatment by deriving intensity conservation explicitly.

Why This Matters

ASTR 230 used luminosity, flux, and the inverse-square law as given formulas. This course rebuilds them from energy conservation so that every later result (blackbody radiation, radiative transfer, hydrostatic equilibrium) rests on the same small set of first principles rather than a list of memorized relations.

Opening Phenomenon

A source of luminosity L radiates energy through every sphere surrounding it at the same total rate, yet the flux (power per unit area) falls as 1/d^2 because the sphere’s area grows as d^2 while the enclosed power does not grow at all — this single geometric fact, not any property of light itself, is the entire content of the inverse-square law.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what follows only once a physical law is derived and applied?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Integrating flux F = L/(4πd²) over any sphere of radius d centered on an isotropic source returns exactly L, independent of d — the defining consistency check for the inverse-square law.
  • A telescope’s measured surface brightness (intensity) of a resolved object, such as the solar disk, does not change with the telescope’s distance from the Sun (ignoring atmospheric and instrumental effects), while the Sun’s total flux clearly does fall with distance — intensity and flux are physically distinct quantities.
  • Doubling the distance to an unresolved point source (a star) always reduces its flux by a factor of exactly 4, independent of the star’s spectral type, luminosity, or composition, confirming the purely geometric origin of the inverse-square law.

Derivation and Model

  • Specific intensity I_ν (energy per unit time, area, solid angle, and frequency interval) is conserved along a ray in vacuum: it does not fall off with distance, because both the emitting and receiving areas subtend proportionally smaller solid angles as distance grows, exactly canceling.
  • Flux F is the intensity integrated over solid angle; for an isotropic point source of luminosity L, the flux measured on a sphere of radius d is F = L/(4πd²), because the same total power L is spread over a sphere of surface area 4πd².
  • Luminosity is the total power radiated in all directions; it is a property of the source alone, while flux additionally depends on the observer’s distance.

Quantitative Tool

\[ F(d) = \dfrac{L}{4\pi d^{2}}, \qquad \oint_{\text{sphere}} F(d)\,dA = L \ \ (\text{independent of } d) \]

Worked Example

  1. Take a source of luminosity L = 3.828e+26 W (the Sun) and evaluate flux at d = 1 AU = 1.496e+11 m: F = L/(4πd²) = 1361.1 W/m², matching the standard quoted solar constant of about 1361 W/m².
  2. At d = 2 AU: F = 340.3 W/m², exactly one quarter of the 1 AU value, confirming the 1/d² scaling numerically rather than merely asserting it.
  3. Total power crossing a sphere of radius 2 AU: F(2 AU) × 4π(2 AU)² = 3.828e+26 W, identical (up to rounding) to L itself, verifying energy conservation across the larger sphere.

Common Misconception

Treating "brightness" as a single unambiguous quantity. A resolved object’s surface brightness (intensity) and an unresolved source’s total flux behave completely differently with distance; conflating them leads to errors whenever the observed source begins to be resolved (e.g., nearby versus distant nebulae of the same physical brightness).

Active Learning Segment

Given two identical light bulbs, one twice as far away as the other, predict the flux ratio without a calculator using only the inverse-square scaling, then verify with the formula.

Synthesis

Flux, intensity, and luminosity are three distinct radiative quantities related by simple geometry; keeping them distinct is the foundation for every quantitative radiative argument later in this course.
