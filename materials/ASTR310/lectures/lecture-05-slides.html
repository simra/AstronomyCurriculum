ASTR 310 · Lecture 05

Hydrostatic Equilibrium: What Holds a Star Up

Deriving, not asserting, the balance between pressure and gravity

Learning Goals

  1. Derive the equation of hydrostatic equilibrium from a force balance on a thin spherical shell of stellar gas.
  2. Use the equation to obtain an order-of-magnitude estimate of a star’s central pressure from its mass and radius alone.
  3. Explain why hydrostatic equilibrium, not fusion, is the immediate reason a star does not collapse or explode on human timescales.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 15.4-15.5 (the Sun’s interior, energy transport); this course derives the governing equation that OpenStax states only descriptively.

Why This Matters

ASTR 230 stated hydrostatic equilibrium as a one-line description. This course derives it as a force-balance equation and uses it quantitatively, which is what makes it possible to build an actual (if approximate) stellar interior model rather than just asserting that "pressure balances gravity."

Opening Phenomenon

The Sun has neither collapsed under its own gravity nor blown itself apart in the roughly 4.6 billion years since it reached the main sequence, despite having no rigid structure at all — every layer of solar gas is in force balance to a precision of better than one part in a million at any instant, or the Sun would visibly expand or contract on short timescales.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what follows only once a physical law is derived and applied?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Helioseismology (analysis of the Sun’s natural oscillation modes) directly measures the Sun’s internal pressure, density, and sound-speed profile, and finds it consistent with standard hydrostatic solar models to a precision of much better than 1%.
  • No star has ever been observed to visibly expand or contract on a dynamical (free-fall) timescale while on the main sequence, exactly as expected if hydrostatic equilibrium holds at every radius.
  • Stars of very different mass (and hence very different central pressure and temperature, by this lecture’s scaling) show a continuous, orderly main sequence in the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram rather than a discontinuous jump, consistent with hydrostatic equilibrium applying smoothly across the full mass range.

Derivation and Model

  • Consider a thin spherical shell of gas at radius r, thickness dr, and density ρ(r) inside a star. The net outward force from the pressure difference across the shell must exactly balance the inward gravitational force from all the mass interior to r.
  • Writing this force balance per unit volume gives the hydrostatic equilibrium equation dP/dr = -G M(r) ρ(r) / r², where M(r) is the mass enclosed within radius r; the pressure must therefore decrease outward everywhere inside a star in equilibrium.
  • Combining this with the ideal gas law P = (ρ k T)/(μ m_p) links pressure, density, and temperature, so hydrostatic equilibrium alone (without yet invoking energy transport or generation) already constrains how P, ρ, and T must vary together inside a star.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \dfrac{dP}{dr} = -\dfrac{G M(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^{2}}, \qquad P_c \sim \dfrac{3}{8\pi}\dfrac{GM^{2}}{R^{4}}\ \ (\text{uniform-density estimate}) \]

Worked Example

  1. Order-of-magnitude central pressure of the Sun from P_c ≈ (3/8π) GM²/R⁴: M = 1.989e+30 kg, R = 6.957e+08 m, giving P_c ≈ 1.345e+14 Pa.
  2. A detailed standard solar model gives a central pressure of about 2.477e+16 Pa (a standard published solar-interior value, not independently re-verified this session); the simple uniform-density estimate above is within a factor of 184.1 of the full numerical solution, which is the expected level of agreement for a one-line order-of-magnitude estimate that ignores the Sun’s actual (strongly centrally concentrated) density profile.
  3. For comparison, sea-level atmospheric pressure on Earth is only about 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa: the Sun’s central pressure exceeds it by a factor of roughly 1.35e+09, illustrating just how large the gravitational confinement of a star’s own mass really is.

Common Misconception

Treating the uniform-density estimate P_c ≈ (3/8π)GM²/R⁴ as an exact stellar-interior result. It is exact only for a strictly uniform-density sphere, which no real star is; real stars are strongly centrally concentrated, so full numerical integration of the hydrostatic equation with a realistic density profile is needed for precision work, as this lecture’s comparison to the standard solar model shows.

Active Learning Segment

Using dP/dr = -GM(r)ρ(r)/r², explain in words why the pressure gradient must be steepest (most negative) near the stellar center, where M(r) and ρ(r) are both largest relative to r.

Synthesis

Hydrostatic equilibrium is a force-balance equation, dP/dr = -GM(r)ρ(r)/r², not merely a qualitative statement, and even a crude uniform-density estimate from it reproduces a star’s central pressure to the correct order of magnitude.
