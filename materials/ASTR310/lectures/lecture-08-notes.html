Lecture 08: The Two-Body Problem: Deriving Kepler’s Laws from Newtonian Gravity

ASTR 310 Astrophysics I: Radiation, Matter, and Orbits

Context and Why This Matters

Kepler’s three laws were originally empirical fits to Tycho Brahe’s planetary position data. Newton’s achievement was to show they follow necessarily from an inverse-square gravitational force law applied to any two bodies, not just the Sun and a planet — which is exactly what makes the same mathematics apply, unchanged, to binary stars, moons, and artificial satellites.

Learning Goals

  1. Reduce the general two-body gravitational problem to an equivalent one-body problem using the reduced mass.
  2. State (without full variational derivation) why an inverse-square central force produces a closed elliptical orbit, and derive the generalized form of Kepler’s third law.
  3. Apply the generalized Kepler’s third law to a real, precisely measured binary star system and cross-check the result against independently measured masses.

Opening Phenomenon

Alpha Centauri A and B, live-verified this session via a direct fetch of their measured orbital elements, orbit their common center of mass with a period of 79.762 years and a semimajor axis of 23.299 AU — numbers measured purely from the stars’ positions on the sky over decades, with no direct measurement of either star’s mass.

Evidence

  • Every planet in the solar system, every known binary star system, and every artificial satellite obeys the same generalized form of Kepler’s third law once both orbiting masses (not just the Sun’s) are included, confirming its origin in Newtonian gravity rather than any property special to planets.
  • Binary star systems with orbits precisely measured over decades (as Alpha Centauri AB has been since Jean Richaud’s recognition of its binary nature in 1689) provide a purely gravitational, model-independent measurement of the sum of both stars’ masses, entirely independent of any stellar-structure or spectral assumptions.
  • The total mass obtained from applying Kepler’s third law to Alpha Centauri AB’s orbit agrees with the sum of the two stars’ individually measured masses (from independent interferometric and asteroseismic methods) to high precision, cross-validating two completely different measurement techniques.

Derivation and Model

  • The full two-body problem (two masses m1 and m2 orbiting their mutual center of mass under their mutual gravity) can be reduced exactly to an equivalent one-body problem: a fictitious particle of reduced mass μ = m1m2/(m1+m2) orbiting a fixed center under the combined mass M = m1+m2, at the actual relative separation r between the two bodies.
  • Solving the resulting one-body problem for an inverse-square force shows the orbit is a conic section (an ellipse, for a bound orbit) with the total mass M at one focus, which is Kepler’s first law, now derived rather than merely observed.
  • Applying Newton’s second law to the reduced one-body problem for a circular orbit (or, more generally, integrating the full elliptical solution) yields the generalized Kepler’s third law, P² = 4π²a³/[G(m1+m2)], which reduces to the Sun-planet form only when m1 >> m2.

Working Equation

\[ \mu = \dfrac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2}, \qquad P^{2} = \dfrac{4\pi^{2}a^{3}}{G\,(m_1+m_2)} \]

Worked Example

  1. Alpha Centauri AB: P = 79.762 yr = 2.5171e+09 s, a = 23.299 AU = 3.4855e+12 m.
  2. Solving Kepler’s third law for the total mass: (m1+m2) = 4π²a³/(GP²) = 1.9877 M☉.
  3. Independently measured individual masses (interferometry + asteroseismology): M_A = 1.0788 M☉, M_B = 0.9092 M☉, summing to 1.9880 M☉.
  4. Agreement between the orbit-based (Kepler) total mass and the independently measured sum: 0.02% — two completely independent techniques (pure celestial mechanics versus interferometric radius/asteroseismic mass modeling) agree to well within their combined measurement uncertainties, directly confirming Newtonian gravity’s two-body prediction for a real system.

Common Misconception

Applying the Sun-planet form of Kepler’s third law (P² = a³, in years and AU) to a binary star system without including both masses. That simplified form silently assumes m2 << m1 (true for a planet around the Sun, false for two comparable-mass stars); using it for Alpha Centauri AB would give a total mass of exactly 1 M☉, badly wrong compared to the correct value of about 2 M☉.

Active-Learning Guidance

Using P² = 4π²a³/[G(m1+m2)], predict qualitatively how the inferred total mass changes if the same angular orbit is observed at twice the true distance (so that the true physical semimajor axis a is twice as large for the same measured period), and explain why an accurate distance measurement is essential for binary-star mass determination.
