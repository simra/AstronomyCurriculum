ASTR 410 Lecture 11: Scaling Relations: Tully-Fisher and Galaxy Structure

Galactic Astronomy · study notes

Learning objectives

  • Use scaling relations as empirical constraints and distance tools.
  • Explain the role of luminosity, rotation, and residuals.
  • Audit an inference from observable to physical conclusion.

Lecture arc

Scaling relations compress complex galaxy physics into testable correlations, not universal laws without scatter. Start by asking students what the telescope actually measures. Then introduce luminosity, rotation, and residuals; delay the equation until the geometry and assumptions are visible. Use the visual as an argument: students should identify what is data, what is a model, and what is inferred.

The lecture should return to the one-dimensional worked calculation and ask whether the answer is stable under a plausible change in distance, tracer selection, or model family.

Terms Developed in Context

Tully-Fisher relation

An empirical correlation between a disk galaxy's luminosity or baryonic mass and characteristic rotation speed. It constrains galaxy formation and can provide relative distances when calibrated. A common form is \(L\propto v_c^\alpha\), with slope and scatter depending on observable choices. In this lecture, use this term while reasoning about luminosity, rotation, and residuals, and distinguish the measured quantity from the model-dependent quantity in \(L\propto v_c^\alpha\). It is a statistical relation, not an exact law for every galaxy. This boundary is important when comparing tracers or carrying the result into the worked example.

Characteristic rotation speed

A consistently defined velocity measure, often from the flat part or width of a rotation curve. It is the kinematic coordinate of the Tully-Fisher relation. Inclination-corrected spectral line width can serve as a proxy for \(v_c\). In this lecture, use this term while reasoning about luminosity, rotation, and residuals, and distinguish the measured quantity from the model-dependent quantity in \(L\propto v_c^\alpha\). Mixing velocity definitions changes the fitted slope and scatter. This boundary is important when comparing tracers or carrying the result into the worked example.

Mass-to-light ratio

The ratio of inferred mass to emitted luminosity in a specified band. It links observed light to stellar mass and helps interpret scaling-relation residuals. Population synthesis predicts stellar mass-to-light ratio from age, metallicity, and IMF assumptions. In this lecture, use this term while reasoning about luminosity, rotation, and residuals, and distinguish the measured quantity from the model-dependent quantity in \(L\propto v_c^\alpha\). It is band-dependent and does not automatically include dark matter. This boundary is important when comparing tracers or carrying the result into the worked example.

Intrinsic scatter

The astrophysical dispersion around a relation remaining after measurement errors are accounted for. It determines both the physical informativeness and distance precision of a scaling relation. Likelihood models separate observational uncertainty from a population-level scatter term. In this lecture, use this term while reasoning about luminosity, rotation, and residuals, and distinguish the measured quantity from the model-dependent quantity in \(L\propto v_c^\alpha\). Observed scatter is not all intrinsic if errors or selection are misspecified. This boundary is important when comparing tracers or carrying the result into the worked example.

Inclination correction

The geometric conversion from projected line-of-sight rotation to the disk-plane speed. It is essential because a face-on disk shows little Doppler rotation even when rotating rapidly. For an ideal disk, observed amplitude scales approximately as \(v_c\sin i\). In this lecture, use this term while reasoning about luminosity, rotation, and residuals, and distinguish the measured quantity from the model-dependent quantity in \(L\propto v_c^\alpha\). Small inclination errors become severe near face-on orientation. This boundary is important when comparing tracers or carrying the result into the worked example.

Derivation and interpretation

\(L\propto v_c^\alpha\)

Define the symbols and units aloud. The relation is useful because it turns an observable into a dynamical or population constraint, but it is conditional on the stated approximation. In discussion, distinguish a parameter that is directly measured from one that is inferred after adopting a model.

Misconceptions and evidence

Selection, inclination, and bandpass can tilt or broaden an observed relation. A map or fitted curve is not a direct inventory. Ask students to list a calibration, selection, or projection effect that could mimic the displayed trend. Then connect the answer to the paired data activity.

Study guidance

Students should be able to reproduce the calculation, explain the approximation, and compare the result with at least one independent tracer.
