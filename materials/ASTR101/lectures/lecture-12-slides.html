Evidence, model, and quantitative reasoning

Why This Question Matters

The Curtis-Shapley debate and Hubble's distance measurements moved galaxies from 'spiral nebulae' to island universes. Morphology became a clue, not the whole story.

Opening Phenomenon

Galaxies are not isolated static islands. Their shapes, colors, star formation, and activity carry evidence of environment and history.

First question: what is directly observed, and what part is an explanation built from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

spiral galaxy

Definition: A galaxy with a rotating stellar disk, gas and dust, and often spiral arms surrounding a central bulge.

Why it matters here: Its structure connects angular momentum, ongoing star formation, and environmental history.

Relationships: Spirals tend to contain colder gas and bluer young populations than many ellipticals.

Boundary or common confusion: Morphology alone does not specify mass, age, or a fixed evolutionary stage.

elliptical galaxy

Definition: A galaxy with a smooth ellipsoidal light distribution and little obvious disk or spiral structure.

Why it matters here: Ellipticals provide evidence about stellar aging, mergers, gas loss, and pressure-supported dynamics.

Relationships: Their stars commonly have more randomly oriented motions than stars in a cold rotating disk.

Boundary or common confusion: Elliptical does not mean featureless or uniformly old, and some retain gas, dust, or recent star formation.

irregular galaxy

Definition: A galaxy lacking a clear spiral or elliptical form at the observed scale and wavelength.

Why it matters here: Irregular structure can expose tidal disturbance, intense star formation, or low-mass disk dynamics.

Relationships: Some irregulars result from interactions, while others are isolated dwarf systems.

Boundary or common confusion: The label describes appearance, not one formation mechanism.

redshift

Definition: The fractional increase in observed wavelength relative to a laboratory or emitted reference wavelength.

Why it matters here: It measures line-of-sight motion at low speeds and traces cosmic expansion for distant galaxies.

Relationships: It is defined by \(z=(\lambda_{\rm obs}-\lambda_0)/\lambda_0\); at small \(z\), \(v\approx cz\).

Boundary or common confusion: Large cosmological redshift is not adequately described as an ordinary speed through static space.

\[ z=\frac{\lambda_{\rm obs}-\lambda_0}{\lambda_0} \]

starburst

Definition: An episode of unusually intense star formation relative to a galaxy's past average or available gas.

Why it matters here: Starbursts rapidly transform gas, dust, luminosity, chemical enrichment, and feedback.

Relationships: Interactions and mergers can compress gas and trigger bursts; massive young stars then produce ultraviolet light and supernovae.

Boundary or common confusion: Bright infrared or ultraviolet emission can be obscured or contaminated by an active nucleus.

active galactic nucleus

Definition: A compact, unusually luminous galactic center powered by matter accreting onto a supermassive black hole.

Why it matters here: AGN can outshine their host galaxies and influence surrounding gas through radiation, winds, and jets.

Relationships: Accretion converts gravitational energy into radiation; orientation and obscuration help explain observed AGN classes.

Boundary or common confusion: A central point source is not automatically an AGN, and black-hole mass alone does not set luminosity without fuel.

merger

Definition: The gravitational interaction and eventual combination of two or more galaxies.

Why it matters here: Mergers rearrange stellar orbits, compress gas, trigger star formation or AGN activity, and build galactic structure.

Relationships: Tidal tails and distorted morphologies are evidence of interaction; dark-matter halos strongly affect the dynamics.

Boundary or common confusion: Most stars do not collide directly because stellar separations are enormous, even though the galaxies merge.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Galaxy colors and spectra trace stellar populations and star formation.
  • Tidal tails, bridges, and disturbed shapes reveal interactions.
  • Redshift connects spectra to motion and, in cosmology, distance scale.

Physical or Geometric Model

  • Morphology is a useful description but not a complete evolutionary sequence.
  • Interactions redistribute gas and stars and can trigger star formation.
  • Supermassive black hole accretion can produce active galactic nuclei.

Quantitative Tool

\[ z = \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}} - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0} \]

At low redshift, redshift can approximate recession speed; at larger redshift, cosmology matters.

Worked Example

  1. A spectral line with rest wavelength 500.7 nm observed at 550.8 nm has z about 0.10.
  2. At low redshift, this corresponds approximately to recession speed 0.10c.
  3. For larger redshift, cosmological interpretation requires a more careful model.

The answer should explain when the low-redshift approximation is adequate and when it is not.

Visual Reasoning

Use morphology, color, spectra, and environment as evidence for galaxy history, not as labels alone.

  • Which features indicate interaction?
  • What evidence traces recent star formation?
  • Why is morphology not a full evolutionary history?

Common Misconception

The Hubble tuning fork is a classification diagram, not a simple evolutionary path from one type to another.

Correct it by treating morphology as classification, not a required evolutionary sequence.
