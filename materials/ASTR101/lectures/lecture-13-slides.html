Why This Question Matters

Slipher's redshifts, Hubble and Lemaitre's expansion interpretation, and the discovery of the cosmic microwave background transformed cosmology from speculation into measurement.

Opening Phenomenon

On the largest scales, galaxies trace an expanding universe with a measurable history, composition, and structure.

First question: what is directly observed, and what part is an explanation built from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

expansion

Definition: The time-dependent increase of distances between sufficiently separated, unbound cosmic locations as described by the changing cosmological scale factor.

Why it matters here: It organizes the redshift-distance relation and the thermal history of the universe.

Relationships: The Hubble parameter is \(\dot a/a\); redshift links observed wavelength to the scale factor at emission.

Boundary or common confusion: Expansion does not make bound systems such as atoms, people, or the solar system grow.

\[ H(t)=\frac{\dot a}{a} \]

Hubble-Lemaitre law

Definition: The approximate proportionality between recession velocity and distance for nearby galaxies participating in cosmic expansion.

Why it matters here: Its slope measures the present expansion rate and provides a first distance-redshift tool.

Relationships: The low-redshift form is \(v=H_0d\); scatter includes peculiar velocities and distance errors.

Boundary or common confusion: It is a local approximation and must be replaced by a cosmological model at large redshift.

\[ v=H_0d \]

cosmic microwave background

Definition: Nearly uniform microwave radiation released when the early universe became transparent, now cooled by expansion.

Why it matters here: Its spectrum and tiny anisotropies provide precise evidence about the early universe and cosmological parameters.

Relationships: Expansion stretches the radiation to a present blackbody temperature near 2.7 K; anisotropies seed later structure.

Boundary or common confusion: It is not light from the Big Bang's first instant and does not come from one distant shell centered on Earth.

lookback time

Definition: The elapsed cosmic time between emission of the light we observe and its reception now.

Why it matters here: It makes distant observations a record of earlier stages of cosmic history.

Relationships: Lookback time depends on redshift and the expansion history, not simply distance divided by today's Hubble speed.

Boundary or common confusion: It is not the current age of the observed object or its present-day distance.

dark matter

Definition: A nonluminous gravitating matter component that clusters and helps build galaxies and larger structures.

Why it matters here: Cosmological structure grows too slowly from the observed early fluctuations without a dominant cold matter component.

Relationships: CMB anisotropies, lensing, galaxy clustering, and galaxy dynamics constrain its abundance and distribution.

Boundary or common confusion: Its particle nature remains unknown, and it is distinct from dark energy.

dark energy

Definition: The name for the component or effect associated with the observed accelerated expansion of the universe.

Why it matters here: It dominates the present cosmic energy budget in the standard model and changes distance-redshift relations.

Relationships: Supernova distances, CMB constraints, and large-scale clustering jointly constrain its behavior.

Boundary or common confusion: The label does not identify a known substance; a cosmological constant is the simplest model, not a settled mechanism.

large-scale structure

Definition: The cosmic network of galaxies, groups, clusters, filaments, walls, and voids on millions to billions of light-years.

Why it matters here: Its statistics test how primordial fluctuations grew under gravity and expansion.

Relationships: Dark matter supplies the main gravitational framework; galaxy surveys trace it with selection-dependent bias.

Boundary or common confusion: Survey maps are incomplete observational samples, not direct three-dimensional pictures of all matter.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Distant galaxies show systematic redshifts.
  • The cosmic microwave background is nearly uniform but has tiny structure.
  • Galaxy clustering and lensing reveal matter distribution across cosmic time.

Physical or Geometric Model

  • Expansion relates redshift and distance, but recession is not ordinary motion through static space.
  • The Big Bang model describes a hot dense early state, not an explosion into pre-existing space.
  • Modern cosmology fits multiple evidence streams with a small set of parameters.

Quantitative Tool

\[ v = H_0 d \]

Use the Hubble law as a low-redshift linear model and look for scatter from peculiar motion and uncertainty.

Worked Example

  1. Using H0 = 70 km/s/Mpc, a galaxy at 100 Mpc has approximate recession speed 7000 km/s.
  2. This low-redshift calculation is a first model, not a complete distance-redshift relation.
  3. Uncertainty in distance and peculiar velocity affects the inference.

The answer should state that the calculation is an approximation and name one source of scatter.

Visual Reasoning

Connect redshift, distance, expansion, and model limits; identify where simple Hubble-law reasoning stops.

  • What does the slope represent?
  • Where would peculiar velocities add scatter?
  • Why is this only a low-redshift approximation?

Common Misconception

The universe does not have a center in the simple expanding-space model; every distant observer sees large-scale expansion.

Correct it by focusing on expansion of space and the absence of a privileged center.

Active Learning Segment
