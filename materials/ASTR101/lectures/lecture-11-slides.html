Opening Phenomenon

First question: what is directly observed, and what part is an explanation built from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

disk

Definition: The flattened rotating component of the Milky Way containing much of its gas, dust, and ongoing star formation.

Why it matters here: It is the Galaxy component in which the Sun resides and where spiral structure is most visible.

Relationships: The disk includes thin and thick stellar populations and surrounds the central bulge.

Boundary or common confusion: It is not razor-thin or perfectly circular, and dust obscures optical views through it.

bulge

Definition: The dense, centrally concentrated stellar component of a disk galaxy.

Why it matters here: Its stars and motions reveal the Milky Way's formation history and central gravitational environment.

Relationships: The Milky Way's bulge is associated with a bar and surrounds the central supermassive black hole.

Boundary or common confusion: A bulge is not simply a scaled-up spherical star cluster; it can be barred, boxy, and dynamically complex.

halo

Definition: A roughly extended, low-density component surrounding the Galaxy's disk and bulge.

Why it matters here: It contains old stars and globular clusters and is embedded in a much more massive dark-matter halo.

Relationships: Halo-star orbits differ from orderly disk rotation; streams record disrupted satellites.

Boundary or common confusion: The sparse visible stellar halo is not the same thing as the dominant dark-matter halo.

spiral arm

Definition: A curved region in a disk galaxy with enhanced gas, dust, young stars, and star formation.

Why it matters here: Spiral arms organize where recent star formation is seen and help map galactic structure.

Relationships: They are commonly treated as patterns or waves through which stars and gas move, not permanent material spokes.

Boundary or common confusion: Visible bright stars overemphasize recent star formation and do not trace all of the disk's mass.

interstellar medium

Definition: The gas, dust, cosmic rays, magnetic fields, and radiation occupying space between stars.

Why it matters here: It supplies material for new stars and receives energy and enriched elements from stellar winds and supernovae.

Relationships: Its cold clouds, warm phases, and hot plasma participate in a star-formation feedback cycle.

Boundary or common confusion: Interstellar space is not empty, but its density is far below ordinary laboratory vacua and varies enormously.

rotation curve

Definition: A graph of orbital speed versus distance from a galaxy's center.

Why it matters here: It tests how enclosed gravitating mass changes with radius.

Relationships: For circular motion, \(v^2=GM(<r)/r\); flat outer curves imply increasing enclosed mass beyond most visible light.

Boundary or common confusion: Interpreting a curve assumes orbital geometry and must account for bars, warps, gas motions, and measurement uncertainties.

dark matter

Definition: Matter inferred from gravity that does not emit, absorb, or scatter enough electromagnetic radiation to be seen directly.

Why it matters here: It explains a large share of the gravitational mass in galaxies, clusters, and cosmic structure models.

Relationships: Rotation curves, gravitational lensing, cluster dynamics, and cosmology provide independent evidence.

Boundary or common confusion: Dark matter names an inferred component, not a confirmed particle identity; ordinary faint matter cannot explain all observations.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • The Milky Way appears as a band because we observe from within its disk.
  • Dust blocks visible light but radio and infrared observations reveal hidden structure.
  • Rotation speeds imply more mass than visible stars and gas can explain.

Physical or Geometric Model

  • The Galaxy contains multiple structural components with different stellar populations.
  • Gas, dust, and star formation trace spiral structure imperfectly.
  • Mass inside an orbit can be inferred from orbital speed under simplifying assumptions.

Quantitative Tool

\[ v^2 \approx \frac{GM(r)}{r} \]

A flat rotation curve means enclosed mass keeps increasing even where visible light fades.

Worked Example

  1. If orbital speed remains flat as radius grows, enclosed mass must keep increasing roughly with radius.
  2. Visible matter does not rise that way at large radii.
  3. The mismatch is one line of evidence for dark matter.

The answer should state what the rotation curve implies about enclosed mass.

Visual Reasoning

Distinguish what is visible from inside the disk from what must be inferred using maps, wavelengths, and rotation.

  • Which components are disk, bulge, and arms?
  • Where is the Sun's embedded viewpoint?
  • What cannot be learned from visible light alone?

Common Misconception

A visible-light photograph is not a complete map of the Galaxy; wavelength determines what structures are visible.

Correct it by comparing visible, infrared, and radio tracers of the Galaxy.
