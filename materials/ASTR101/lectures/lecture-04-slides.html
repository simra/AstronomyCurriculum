Why This Question Matters

Tycho Brahe's precise observations made Kepler's laws possible; Newton then showed that the same gravity acting on falling objects also governs the Moon and planets. The formula followed the data.

Opening Phenomenon

Planets do not move randomly: they sweep out repeatable paths that reveal how gravity organizes motion from moons to galaxies.

First question: what is directly observed, and what part is an explanation built from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

ellipse

Definition: A closed curve for which the sum of distances to two fixed foci is constant.

Why it matters here: Bound two-body orbits are ellipses in the Newtonian approximation.

Relationships: The central mass lies at one focus; semi-major axis sets orbital scale and eccentricity sets shape.

Boundary or common confusion: Real multi-body orbits are perturbed and need not remain perfect fixed ellipses.

semi-major axis

Definition: Half the longest diameter of an ellipse.

Why it matters here: For a bound orbit it is the characteristic size that enters Kepler's third law and the orbital energy.

Relationships: Orbital period grows as \(a^{3/2}\) for bodies orbiting the same dominant mass.

Boundary or common confusion: It is not the instantaneous distance from the orbiting body to the focus except for a circular orbit.

\[ P^2\propto a^3 \]

period

Definition: The time required for a repeating motion to complete one cycle.

Why it matters here: Orbital period is directly observable and constrains system mass when paired with orbital size.

Relationships: Kepler's third law connects period to semi-major axis and total gravitating mass.

Boundary or common confusion: Different periods can be defined relative to the stars, the Sun, or repeating alignments.

eccentricity

Definition: A dimensionless measure of how much an orbit departs from a circle.

Why it matters here: It controls the contrast between nearest and farthest orbital distances and the associated speed changes.

Relationships: Bound ellipses have \(0\le e<1\); \(e=0\) is circular.

Boundary or common confusion: A diagram can exaggerate eccentricity, and Earth's orbit is much closer to circular than many textbook sketches suggest.

center of mass

Definition: The mass-weighted average position of all matter in a system.

Why it matters here: Two gravitating bodies both orbit their common center of mass rather than one remaining perfectly fixed.

Relationships: Its location obeys \(m_1r_1=m_2r_2\) for two bodies along their separation line.

Boundary or common confusion: It can lie inside one body, between bodies, or even outside both; it is not necessarily a material point.

\[ m_1r_1=m_2r_2 \]

escape velocity

Definition: The minimum launch speed needed to reach arbitrarily large distance with zero speed remaining, neglecting later propulsion and drag.

Why it matters here: It connects a body's mass and radius to atmospheric retention and spacecraft energetics.

Relationships: For a spherical body, \(v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}\).

Boundary or common confusion: It is not the speed needed to remain in orbit, and real launches face atmosphere, rotation, and powered trajectories.

\[ v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} \]

tide

Definition: A differential gravitational effect caused by variation in gravitational acceleration across an extended body.

Why it matters here: Tides deform oceans and solid bodies, exchange angular momentum, heat moons, and can disrupt objects.

Relationships: Tidal strength rises strongly at small separation, approximately as \(M/r^3\) for a distant perturber.

Boundary or common confusion: A tide is not simply the total gravitational pull; uniform acceleration of the whole body does not create stretching.

\[ a_{\rm tidal}\propto \frac{M}{r^3} \]

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Planet positions repeat with periods related to orbital size.
  • Moons, planets, asteroids, and spacecraft all follow the same gravitational principles.
  • Tidal effects depend on differences in gravitational pull across an extended body.

Physical or Geometric Model

  • Kepler's laws summarize orbital patterns; Newton's law explains why they occur.
  • An orbit is free fall with enough sideways motion to continually miss the central body.
  • The two-body model is powerful but approximate; real systems include perturbations.

Quantitative Tool

\[ P^2 = a^3 \quad \text{and} \quad F_g = G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2} \]

Use the solar-system form only with years, AU, and orbits around the Sun; otherwise include the central mass.

Common Misconception

There is gravity in space. Astronauts feel weightless because they are in continuous free fall, not because gravity is absent.

Correct it by describing orbit as continuous free fall around a central mass.

Active Learning Segment

Synthesis

Gravity turns observation into prediction: if the model is right, positions, speeds, and periods are linked quantitatively.
