Why This Question Matters

Kirchhoff, Bunsen, Fraunhofer, and later quantum physicists transformed astronomy by showing that spectra are physical fingerprints. After spectroscopy, stars became laboratories rather than just lights.

Opening Phenomenon

Almost everything we know about distant objects arrives as light. Spectra transform light from a picture into a physical measurement of temperature, composition, and motion.

First question: what is directly observed, and what part is an explanation built from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

wavelength

Definition: The spatial distance over which a wave's pattern repeats.

Why it matters here: Astronomical wavelength selects which physical processes, temperatures, and materials a detector can reveal.

Relationships: In vacuum, wavelength and frequency satisfy \(c=\lambda\nu\); photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength.

Boundary or common confusion: Color names cover broad bands, and measured wavelength can be shifted by source motion or cosmic expansion.

\[ c=\lambda\nu \]

frequency

Definition: The number of wave cycles passing a point per unit time.

Why it matters here: Frequency is directly linked to photon energy and provides an alternative coordinate for spectra.

Relationships: It increases as wavelength decreases in vacuum, and \(E=h\nu\).

Boundary or common confusion: Wavelength and frequency axes run in opposite directions, so spectral trends must be read carefully.

\[ E=h\nu \]

photon

Definition: A quantum of electromagnetic radiation carrying energy and momentum.

Why it matters here: Detectors ultimately count or convert photons, so astronomical evidence is limited by photon statistics.

Relationships: Photon energy is \(E=h\nu=hc/\lambda\), linking quantum detections to spectral wavelength.

Boundary or common confusion: A photon is not a tiny colored pellet following a classical trajectory; quantum behavior depends on the measurement.

\[ E=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

blackbody

Definition: An ideal absorber and emitter whose thermal spectrum depends only on temperature.

Why it matters here: It provides the baseline model for interpreting stellar continua and planetary thermal emission.

Relationships: Wien's law links peak wavelength to temperature, while Stefan-Boltzmann links emitted flux to \(T^4\).

Boundary or common confusion: Real spectra contain absorption, emission, opacity effects, and geometric complications.

\[ \lambda_{\max}T=b \]

emission line

Definition: A narrow spectral feature where intensity exceeds the surrounding continuum because atoms, ions, or molecules emit at particular transitions.

Why it matters here: Emission lines identify material and diagnose temperature, density, ionization, and motion.

Relationships: The line wavelength reflects an energy-level difference; Doppler shifts move the observed wavelength.

Boundary or common confusion: A line's strength is not a simple abundance meter because excitation, opacity, and geometry also matter.

absorption line

Definition: A narrow reduction in intensity where intervening material removes photons at specific transition wavelengths.

Why it matters here: Stellar absorption lines reveal atmospheric composition, temperature class, pressure, and radial motion.

Relationships: They occur when cooler gas lies in front of a brighter continuous source and share transition wavelengths with corresponding emission lines.

Boundary or common confusion: A missing or weak line does not prove an element is absent; the atoms may be in the wrong excitation or ionization state.

Doppler shift

Definition: A change in observed wavelength or frequency caused by relative line-of-sight motion between source and observer.

Why it matters here: It measures radial velocity in stars, gas clouds, galaxies, and exoplanet-host systems.

Relationships: At speeds much less than light, \(\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0\approx v_r/c\).

Boundary or common confusion: It measures only the line-of-sight component, and cosmological redshift requires a broader expansion model at large distances.

\[ \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0}\approx\frac{v_r}{c} \]

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Hot dense objects produce continuous spectra whose peak color depends on temperature.
  • Thin gases emit or absorb light at characteristic wavelengths.
  • Shifted spectral lines reveal radial motion.

Physical or Geometric Model

  • Light behaves as both wave and photon depending on the measurement.
  • Atoms have quantized energy levels, producing diagnostic line spectra.
  • A spectrum must be calibrated before line position or intensity can be trusted.

Quantitative Tool

\[ c = \lambda\nu \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0} \approx \frac{v_r}{c} \]

A line shift is meaningful only after identifying the rest wavelength and preserving the sign of the shift.

Worked Example

  1. A line with rest wavelength 656.3 nm observed at 657.0 nm is redshifted.
  2. The fractional shift is 0.7/656.3, about 0.00107.
  3. The radial speed is therefore about 320 km/s away from the observer.

The answer should identify redshift or blueshift, compute the speed, and interpret the direction of motion.

Common Misconception

Color alone is not a complete temperature measurement; filters, extinction, detector response, and calibration matter.

Correct it by naming calibration, extinction, filters, and detector response as parts of color measurement.
