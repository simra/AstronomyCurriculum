The Habitable Zone: Radiative-Equilibrium Boundaries

Deriving where liquid water can, in principle, persist on a surface

Learning Goals

  1. Derive the classical (conservative) habitable-zone inner and outer flux boundaries from radiative-equilibrium and greenhouse physics.
  2. Compute the habitable zone for the Sun and for TRAPPIST-1 and explain why it scales so differently with stellar luminosity.
  3. Critically evaluate which real planets in this course’s dataset fall within, near, or outside their star’s habitable zone.

Why This Matters

Every detection, demographic, interior, atmospheric, and escape tool developed so far (Lectures 01-08) converges here: the habitable zone is the first, most basic astronomical filter applied to ask whether a given real planet could, in principle, host surface liquid water -- the necessary (not sufficient) condition this course’s entire second half builds on.

Opening Phenomenon

The Sun’s classical habitable zone spans roughly 0.95 to 1.77 AU (Earth, at 1.00 AU, sits comfortably inside it) -- but TRAPPIST-1, a star roughly 1800 times fainter than the Sun, has a habitable zone spanning only 0.0227 to 0.0421 AU, closer to its star than Mercury is to the Sun, packing three to four of its seven known planets into a habitable zone that would fit inside Mercury’s own orbit.

First question: what here is directly observed or a published/live-verified measurement, and what follows only once a physical law is derived and applied?

Vocabulary for Reasoning

habitable zone

runaway greenhouse (moist-greenhouse) limit

maximum-greenhouse limit

circumstellar habitable zone

insolation (stellar flux)

zero-albedo equilibrium temperature

Use these terms to describe derivations and evidence, not as isolated definitions.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Radiative-transfer climate models (beginning with Kasting, Whitmire & Reynolds 1993 and refined by many subsequent studies) computing the surface temperature and water-vapor content of an Earth-like atmosphere as a function of received stellar flux find two critical flux thresholds: above roughly 1.1 times Earth’s insolation, water vapor becomes a runaway greenhouse gas and the whole ocean can evaporate; below roughly 0.32 times Earth’s insolation, CO2 condenses out of the atmosphere before it can trap enough heat to keep the surface above freezing.
  • Venus (at 0.72 AU, receiving roughly 1.9 times Earth’s insolation) sits well inside the classical inner boundary and today has no surface liquid water and a runaway-greenhouse-scale 737 K surface temperature, while Mars (at 1.52 AU, receiving roughly 0.43 times Earth’s insolation) sits near but just outside the classical outer boundary and today has no stable surface liquid water either -- both real solar-system planets consistent with the classical HZ boundaries this lecture derives.
  • Multiple TRAPPIST-1 planets (commonly cited as e, f, and sometimes d or g, depending on the specific model’s albedo and greenhouse assumptions) fall within the zero-albedo classical habitable zone computed directly from the star’s live-verified luminosity, the observational fact that made TRAPPIST-1 one of the most intensely studied systems for habitability by JWST since 2022.

Derivation and Model

  • A planet’s received flux (insolation) at orbital distance a is S(a) = L/(4 pi a^2); the habitable zone’s inner and outer edges are defined as the orbital distances at which S(a) equals two critical flux values (in units of Earth’s insolation S_Earth): S_inner ≈ 1.1 S_Earth (the runaway/moist-greenhouse limit) and S_outer ≈ 0.32 S_Earth (the maximum-greenhouse limit), so that d_inner = sqrt(L/S_inner) and d_outer = sqrt(L/S_outer) in AU when L is in solar luminosities.
  • Because flux falls off as 1/d^2, this reduces to a simple luminosity scaling: since S_inner and S_outer are fixed multiples of Earth’s insolation regardless of the host star, the habitable-zone distance for any star scales as d ∝ sqrt(L); a star 1800 times fainter than the Sun (like TRAPPIST-1) therefore has a habitable zone roughly sqrt(1800) ≈ 42 times closer in than the Sun’s, exactly the pattern this lecture’s worked example quantifies.
  • These boundaries are explicitly conservative and one-dimensional (they assume an Earth-like atmospheric composition and water/CO2 greenhouse physics, and they say nothing about a specific planet’s actual atmosphere, rotation, or albedo); Lecture 10 extends this classical picture with climate feedback and alternative habitability criteria that can shift or blur these boundaries in either direction for a real, specific planet.

Quantitative Tool

\[ S(a) = \dfrac{L}{4\pi a^{2}}, \qquad d_{\rm inner,outer} = \sqrt{\dfrac{L/L_\odot}{S_{\rm inner,outer}/S_\oplus}}\ \mathrm{AU} \]

Worked Example

  1. Sun’s habitable zone (L=1 Lsun): d_inner = sqrt(1/1.1) = 0.953 AU, d_outer = sqrt(1/0.32) = 1.768 AU -- Earth (1.00 AU) sits 6% of the way from the inner to the outer edge, comfortably inside.
  2. TRAPPIST-1’s habitable zone (L=5.660e-04 Lsun, live-verified): d_inner = 0.0227 AU, d_outer = 0.0421 AU. Checking TRAPPIST-1e (a=0.0293 AU): it sits 34% of the way from the inner to outer edge -- inside the classical habitable zone, consistent with its status as this system’s most commonly cited habitability candidate.
  3. Checking the rest of the TRAPPIST-1 system against the same computed boundaries: planets b, c, and d (a=0.0115, 0.0158, 0.0223 AU) fall inside the 0.0227 AU inner edge (too close, zero-albedo equilibrium temperatures of 400, 342, 287 K), while g and h (a=0.0469, 0.0619 AU) straddle or fall outside the 0.0421 AU outer edge -- a direct, quantitative habitable-zone census of a real seven-planet system.

Common Misconception

Treating "inside the habitable zone" as synonymous with "habitable" or "has liquid water." The classical habitable zone is a necessary radiative-equilibrium condition computed for an assumed Earth-like atmosphere; Venus’s real, catastrophic runaway greenhouse and Mars’s real, frozen surface show that a planet’s actual climate depends on its specific atmospheric composition, mass, and history, not on orbital distance alone -- exactly why Lecture 10 extends this classical picture rather than treating it as the final word.

Active Learning Segment

Using d ∝ sqrt(L), estimate how much closer in the habitable zone of a star with half the Sun’s luminosity would be compared to the Sun’s own habitable zone, and explain why this scaling makes low-mass M-dwarf systems like TRAPPIST-1 simultaneously the easiest systems in which to detect multiple habitable-zone-candidate planets (Lecture 02’s transit-probability argument) and the hardest in which to be confident those planets have actually retained a life-friendly atmosphere (Lecture 08’s escape argument).

Synthesis

The classical habitable zone, derived from radiative-equilibrium flux balance and Earth-like greenhouse physics, converts a star’s luminosity directly into an orbital-distance range where surface liquid water is radiatively plausible -- a necessary but explicitly not sufficient condition for habitability, as Lecture 10’s climate-feedback extensions and this course’s later biosignature-false-positive discussion (Lecture 11) both make clear.
