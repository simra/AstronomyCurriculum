The Transit Method: Light Curves, Depth, and Duration

Catching a planet in silhouette

Learning Goals

  2. Derive an approximate transit-duration relation and explain what additional information a transit provides beyond radial velocity.
  3. Reproduce the real discovery measurement of HD 209458 b, the first transiting exoplanet, and cross-check it against this course’s computed values.

Why This Matters

Lecture 01’s radial-velocity method gives a planet’s mass (times sin i) but says nothing about its size. The transit method, derived here, supplies the missing radius -- and, combined with radial velocity for the same planet, breaks the sin(i) degeneracy exactly and yields the planet’s true mass, true radius, and therefore its bulk density (Lecture 06).

Opening Phenomenon

On September 9 and 16, 1999, David Charbonneau’s team measured a real, repeatable 1.5% dip in the brightness of HD 209458, lasting about 3 hours every 3.52 days -- the first confirmed transiting exoplanet, simultaneously announced by two independent teams in the same issue of the Astrophysical Journal, and the system this course uses throughout its atmospheres unit (Lectures 07-08).

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • A planet passing in front of its star blocks a fraction of the star’s disk proportional to the ratio of their cross-sectional areas, producing a periodic, repeatable dip in brightness with a shape (flat-bottomed trapezoid) that is a direct geometric consequence of the planet’s finite crossing time, confirmed by high-precision photometry of HD 209458 and thousands of subsequent transiting systems.
  • Only systems with a favorable orbital inclination (viewed nearly edge-on) show transits at all; this geometric requirement is exactly why transiting systems are a minority of all planetary systems, but it is also exactly what makes transiting systems so valuable -- the required near-edge-on inclination (i=86.71 degrees for HD 209458 b) is known precisely from the transit geometry itself.
  • Combining HD 209458 b’s transit-derived radius with its independently measured (radial-velocity) mass gave the first-ever bulk density measurement for a planet outside the solar system, immediately confirming it as a gas giant and not, for example, a brown dwarf or a rocky super-Earth -- a capability radial velocity alone could never provide.

Derivation and Model

  • For a planet of radius Rp transiting a star of radius Rstar, the fractional decrease in observed flux at mid-transit (ignoring stellar limb darkening) is the ratio of blocked area to total area: delta = (Rp/Rstar)^2, a purely geometric relation independent of the planet’s mass or composition.
  • For a central (impact parameter b=0), circular-orbit transit, the total transit duration is approximately T = (P/pi) * arcsin(Rstar/a), because the star subtends an angular half-width of arcsin(Rstar/a) as seen from the planet’s orbital position; more precise treatments add ingress/egress timing and non-zero impact parameter corrections.
  • A transiting system’s inclination is fixed by the transit’s existence (i near 90 degrees, quantifiable from the transit’s duration and shape), so if the same planet is also observed with radial velocity, the sin(i) degeneracy from Lecture 01 is broken exactly, yielding both the planet’s true mass and true radius from the same object -- the reason transiting, RV-confirmed planets like HD 209458 b are the best-characterized exoplanets known.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \delta = \left(\dfrac{R_p}{R_\ast}\right)^{2}, \qquad T_{\rm dur} \approx \dfrac{P}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\dfrac{R_\ast}{a}\right) \]

Worked Example

  1. HD 209458 b (live-verified this session): Rp = 1.359 RJup, Rstar = 1.20 Rsun. Computed transit depth delta = (Rp/Rstar)^2 = 1.30%, matching the discovery team’s measured 1.5% dip to within the precision of the adopted stellar radius.
  2. Computed transit duration from P = 3.525 d and a = 0.0475 AU: T = (P/pi) arcsin(Rstar/a) = 3.17 h, matching the discovery paper’s reported ≈3-hour transit.
  3. Cross-checking with Lecture 01’s method: HD 209458 b’s radial-velocity semi-amplitude, predicted from its independently known true mass (0.682 MJup) and inclination (86.71 degrees, from the transit), gives K = 82.8 m/s, matching the published measured value (84.27 m/s) -- direct confirmation that the two independent detection methods agree on the same real planet.

Common Misconception

Assuming transit depth alone gives a planet’s absolute size. Transit depth only ever gives the ratio Rp/Rstar; converting to an absolute planet radius requires an independently known stellar radius (from the star’s spectral type, parallax-based luminosity, or asteroseismology), so an error in the assumed stellar radius propagates directly and proportionally into every transiting planet’s reported radius.

Active Learning Segment

Using T ∝ P^(1/3) (from a ∝ P^(2/3) via Kepler’s third law) and Rstar/a ∝ a^(-1), explain qualitatively why short-period, close-in planets have both higher transit probability and shorter transit duration than long-period planets, and why this makes hot Jupiters disproportionately easy to detect by transit as well as by radial velocity.

Synthesis

The transit method supplies exactly what radial velocity cannot: a planet’s radius and (via the transit-fixed inclination) an exact, not minimum, mass when combined with radial velocity -- together the two methods gave HD 209458 b the first bulk density ever measured for a world outside the solar system, opening the door to every interior and atmospheric characterization technique this course develops later.
