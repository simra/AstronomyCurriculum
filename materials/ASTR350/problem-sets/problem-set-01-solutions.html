1. Semi-amplitude sanity check

K ∝ P^(-1/3), so doubling P scales K by 2^(-1/3)=0.7937, i.e. K would drop to about 79.4% of its original value, roughly 44.4 m/s instead of 55.9 m/s.

3. HD 209458 b transit depth from scratch

Method (a): delta=(Rp/Rstar)^2 = 1.295%. Method (b): density from mass/radius gives ρ=360 kg/m^3; using M and this density to recover R gives back the same Rp (since R was used to compute the density), so the two methods necessarily agree by construction -- the pedagogical point is recognizing this circularity rather than treating (b) as an independent check.

4. Transit-duration scaling

T ∝ P^(1/3): at P=1.0 d versus the real 3.52 d, T scales by (1.0/3.525)^(1/3)=0.657, giving T≈2.08 h instead of 3.17 h -- a shorter transit is harder to schedule precisely but occurs more frequently (shorter period), a real trade-off in real observing proposals.

5. Combining methods to get bulk density

ρ = 360 kg/m^3 = 0.360 g/cm^3 -- lower than water (1.0 g/cm^3) and far below any solar-system planet in materials/ASTR350/data/, confirming its gas-giant, low-density interior rather than similarity to any specific solar-system body.

Grading Notes

Award full marks for correct method, units, and a numeric result consistent with the arithmetic shown (allow reasonable rounding). Verify that the student re-derives the number for this problem's specific inputs rather than quoting a lecture example without adaptation. Watch specifically for unit-conversion errors (AU/m, days/seconds, Jupiter/Earth mass-radius units), for comparative-magnitude statements given in the wrong direction, and for level-1/level-2/level-3 data-provenance conflation (e.g., presenting an illustrative Miller-Urey bar height as a precise measured yield) -- these are the most common sources of mistakes in this course.
