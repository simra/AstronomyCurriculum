ASTR 230 · Lecture 09

Black Holes and Curved Spacetime

When even degeneracy pressure is not enough

Learning Goals

  1. Compute the Schwarzschild radius for a given mass and explain its physical meaning as an event horizon.
  2. Explain why black holes form when a collapsing core exceeds the maximum mass supportable by neutron degeneracy pressure.
  3. Describe two independent lines of observational evidence for black holes.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 24.1-24.3 (introduction to relativity and time dilation, spacetime, black holes and their observation).

Why This Matters

Neutron degeneracy pressure, like electron degeneracy pressure, has a maximum mass it can support (roughly 2-3 M☉ for neutron stars). Beyond that mass, physics as currently understood allows no known force to halt gravitational collapse, leading to a black hole — once a theoretical curiosity of general relativity, now directly imaged (Event Horizon Telescope, 2019) and detected via gravitational waves from merging black holes (LIGO/Virgo, since 2015).

Opening Phenomenon

The Event Horizon Telescope’s 2019 image of the supermassive black hole in M87 directly showed a dark central shadow surrounded by a bright ring of glowing gas, matching general-relativistic predictions for a black hole’s event horizon and photon ring to within observational precision.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what is inferred from a physical model?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • X-ray binaries such as Cygnus X-1 show a compact object whose mass (measured from its orbit with a visible companion star) exceeds the maximum possible neutron star mass, yet which emits no detectable surface radiation of its own — consistent with a black hole rather than a neutron star.
  • LIGO and Virgo have directly detected gravitational waves from dozens of merging black hole pairs since 2015, with masses and waveforms matching general-relativistic predictions for black hole inspiral and merger.
  • The Event Horizon Telescope imaged the shadow of the supermassive black hole in M87 (2019) and Sagittarius A* at the Milky Way’s center (2022), both consistent with the predicted size for their independently measured masses.

Model

  • A black hole’s event horizon is the boundary within which the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light, so no light or information can escape from inside it.
  • The Schwarzschild radius R_s = 2GM/c² gives the event horizon radius for a non-rotating black hole of mass M; it scales linearly with mass, so more massive black holes have larger (not smaller) event horizons.
  • Black holes form when a collapsing core exceeds the maximum mass supportable by neutron degeneracy pressure (roughly 2-3 M☉); above that, general relativity predicts unstoppable collapse to a singularity cloaked by an event horizon.

Quantitative Tool

\[ R_s = \dfrac{2GM}{c^{2}} \]

Worked Example

  1. For a 10 M☉ stellar-mass black hole: M = 10 × 1.989e+30 kg = 1.989e+31 kg.
  2. R_s = 2 × 6.674e-11 × 1.989e+31 / (2.998e+08)² = 29538.5 m ≈ 29.5 km.
  3. For the Milky Way’s central supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A* (mass ≈ 4.3 × 10⁶ M☉): R_s = 2 × 6.674e-11 × (4.3e6 × 1.989e+30) / (2.998e+08)² ≈ 1.27e+07 km — roughly 430000 times larger than the 10 M☉ case, directly illustrating that R_s scales linearly with mass.

Common Misconception

Imagining a black hole as a "cosmic vacuum cleaner" that pulls in everything nearby with unusually strong gravity. Outside the event horizon, a black hole’s gravity is identical to that of any other object of the same mass; a Sun-mass black hole at the Sun’s current distance would not pull Earth in any harder than the Sun already does.

Active Learning Segment

Compute the Schwarzschild radius for Earth’s mass (5.97 × 10²⁴ kg) and compare it to Earth’s actual radius, to see why Earth is nowhere close to being a black hole.

Synthesis

Black holes are the endpoint of collapse for cores too massive for neutron degeneracy pressure to support, with an event horizon size (Schwarzschild radius) that scales linearly with mass and is now directly probed by gravitational-wave and imaging observations.
