ASTR 230 · Lecture 01

Measuring the Stars: Luminosity, Temperature, and Radius

From a point of light to a physical object

Learning Goals

  1. State the Stefan-Boltzmann law and use it to relate a star’s luminosity, radius, and surface temperature.
  2. Distinguish apparent brightness from luminosity using the inverse-square law.
  3. Verify the Sun’s luminosity from first principles using its measured radius and temperature.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 18.1-18.2 (the brightness of stars, radii of stars) and Chapter 5.4 (Stefan-Boltzmann and Wien’s laws).

Why This Matters

A star is a point of light in every telescope humans have ever built or will build for the foreseeable future — we cannot resolve a stellar surface (except, barely, for a handful of the nearest supergiants with modern interferometry). Everything we know about stellar sizes, temperatures, and total energy output is inferred from the amount and color of light that reaches us, combined with a small number of physical laws. This lecture builds the single most important of those laws.

Opening Phenomenon

The Sun has a measured radius of 6.957e+05 km and a measured surface temperature of 5778 K. Using only these two numbers and the Stefan-Boltzmann law, we can predict its total power output and compare that prediction to the independently measured solar constant.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what is inferred from a physical model?

Vocabulary for Reasoning

luminosity

apparent brightness (flux)

Stefan-Boltzmann law

inverse-square law

effective temperature

blackbody spectrum

Use these terms to describe evidence and relationships, not as isolated definitions.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • The Sun’s directly measured radius is R = 6.957e+08 m and its spectrum peaks at a wavelength consistent with an effective temperature of T = 5778 K.
  • Two stars can have identical apparent brightness in the sky yet radically different luminosities, if one is much farther away (Betelgeuse, at roughly 170 pc, radiates over 100,000 times the Sun’s luminosity yet appears fainter in our sky than much closer, intrinsically dimmer stars).
  • Every star’s continuous spectrum approximates a blackbody curve, whose shape and peak wavelength depend only on temperature (Wien’s law), not on composition or size.

Model

  • The Stefan-Boltzmann law states that a blackbody radiates a power per unit area equal to σT⁴, so a star’s total luminosity is L = 4πR²σT⁴.
  • The inverse-square law states that the flux (apparent brightness) received at distance d falls off as F = L / (4πd²), so the same luminosity looks fainter the farther away it is.
  • Because flux depends on both L and d, we cannot read off a star’s luminosity from its apparent brightness alone — we need an independent distance measurement (Lecture 03).

Quantitative Tool

\[ L = 4\pi R^{2} \sigma T^{4}, \qquad F = \dfrac{L}{4\pi d^{2}} \]

Worked Example

  1. The Sun: R = 6.9570e+08 m, T = 5778 K, σ = 5.6704e-08 W m⁻² K⁻⁴.
  2. L = 4π(6.957e+08)² × (5.670e-08) × (5778)⁴ = 3.844e+26 W.
  3. The independently measured (satellite radiometer) solar luminosity is 3.828e+26 W.
  4. Agreement: the Stefan-Boltzmann prediction differs from the measured value by only 0.42%, confirming the law using the one star whose radius and temperature we can measure directly and whose luminosity we can also measure directly by other means.

Common Misconception

Confusing "bright in the sky" with "luminous." A star’s apparent brightness is a joint function of luminosity and distance; only after fixing the distance can brightness be converted into luminosity.

Active Learning Segment

Given two stars with equal apparent brightness, one twice as far away as the other, compute the luminosity ratio required to produce that equal apparent brightness.

Lab Connection

Lab 01 uses this exact Stefan-Boltzmann relation, together with parallax distances (Lecture 03), to place 20 real stars on a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.

Synthesis

Two physical laws — Stefan-Boltzmann and inverse-square — convert a point of starlight into a physical size, temperature, and total energy output, but only once an independent distance is known.
