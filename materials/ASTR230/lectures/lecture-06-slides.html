Life After the Main Sequence: Giants, Clusters, and Stellar Aging

Why massive stars live fast and die young

Learning Goals

  1. State the mass-luminosity relation and derive the resulting scaling of main-sequence lifetime with mass.
  2. Explain why the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram of a star cluster changes shape as the cluster ages ("main-sequence turnoff").
  3. Estimate a cluster’s age from the mass of its most massive star still on the main sequence.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 22.1-22.3 (evolution from the main sequence to red giants, star clusters, checking out the theory).

Why This Matters

All the stars in a young open cluster form at essentially the same time from the same molecular cloud, but a cluster’s highest-mass stars evolve off the main sequence first, while its lowest-mass stars remain unchanged for tens of billions of years. This "main-sequence turnoff" is one of the most important clocks in astronomy, used to date everything from nearby open clusters to the oldest globular clusters in the Galaxy.

Opening Phenomenon

A 3 solar-mass main-sequence star has roughly 47 times the Sun’s luminosity, yet only 3 times the Sun’s fuel supply — it must burn through its hydrogen far faster relative to its supply than the Sun does.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what is inferred from a physical model?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Observed main-sequence stars show luminosity scaling roughly as the 3.5 power of mass (L ∝ M^3.5) across most of the main sequence, from direct measurements of eclipsing binary systems where both mass and luminosity can be measured independently.
  • Young open clusters (ages of a few million years) still have their most massive O and B stars on the main sequence, while older open clusters (hundreds of millions of years) have already lost their most massive stars to post-main-sequence evolution, leaving a lower-mass "turnoff point."
  • Globular clusters, the oldest stellar populations in the Galaxy (roughly 10-13 billion years), have main-sequence turnoffs at stellar masses below the Sun’s, consistent with their great age.

Model

  • A star’s main-sequence lifetime is proportional to its fuel supply (mass) divided by its fuel consumption rate (luminosity): t ∝ M / L.
  • Combining this with the mass-luminosity relation L ∝ M^3.5 gives t ∝ M / M^3.5 = M^-2.5, so lifetime falls steeply with increasing mass.
  • A cluster’s main-sequence turnoff mass therefore directly measures the cluster’s age: only stars below the turnoff mass are still core-hydrogen-burning; more massive stars have already evolved into giants or died.

Quantitative Tool

\[ L \propto M^{3.5}, \qquad t_{\rm MS} \propto \dfrac{M}{L} \propto M^{-2.5}, \qquad t_{\rm MS} \approx 10\ \mathrm{Gyr} \times \left(\dfrac{M}{M_\odot}\right)^{-2.5} \]

Worked Example

  1. The Sun (1 M☉): predicted main-sequence lifetime = 10 Gyr × 1^-2.5 = 10.0 Gyr, consistent with the Sun’s current age (about 4.6 Gyr) being roughly midway through its main-sequence life.
  2. A 3 M☉ star: L = 3^3.5 = 46.8 L☉; lifetime = 10 Gyr × 3^-2.5 = 0.642 Gyr = 642 Myr.
  3. A 10 M☉ star: lifetime = 10 Gyr × 10^-2.5 ≈ 32 Myr — more than an order of magnitude shorter than the 3 M☉ case, despite having only about 3 times the mass.
  4. If we observe a cluster whose main-sequence turnoff is at 3 M☉ (stars above this mass have already left the main sequence), the cluster’s age is therefore approximately 642 million years — this is exactly the method used in Lab 03.

Common Misconception

Assuming more massive stars live longer because they "have more fuel." They do have more fuel, but they burn it so much faster (L ∝ M^3.5) that the net effect is a much shorter lifetime, not a longer one.

Synthesis

Because luminosity rises much faster than mass along the main sequence, lifetime falls steeply with mass, turning a cluster’s main-sequence turnoff point into a direct measurement of its age.
