ASTR 230 · Lecture 07

The Death of Sun-Like Stars: White Dwarfs and the Chandrasekhar Limit

What is left when fusion stops

Learning Goals

  1. Describe the evolutionary sequence from red giant to planetary nebula to white dwarf for a Sun-like star.
  2. Explain electron degeneracy pressure as the force that supports a white dwarf against gravity.
  3. Compute a white dwarf’s density from its measured mass and radius, and state why the Chandrasekhar limit exists.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 23.1 (the death of low-mass stars).

Why This Matters

Sirius B, the first white dwarf discovered (from its gravitational effect on Sirius A’s motion, long before it could be directly imaged), forced nineteenth- and twentieth-century astronomers to accept that stellar matter can exist in a state with no terrestrial analog: matter compressed to planetary size while retaining roughly a solar mass, supported not by thermal pressure but by a purely quantum-mechanical effect.

Opening Phenomenon

Sirius B has a measured mass of 1.018 M☉ (from its orbit around Sirius A) yet a measured radius of only 0.008098 R☉ — about the size of Earth. Packing a solar mass into an Earth-sized volume implies an extraordinary density.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what is inferred from a physical model?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Sirius B: mass = 1.018 ± 0.011 M☉, radius = 0.008098 R☉ = 5,634 km, effective temperature = 25,000 K, luminosity = 0.0245 L☉ (Bond et al. 2017, Hipparcos parallax of 2.637 pc).
  • No known main-sequence star has anywhere near Sirius B’s combination of solar-scale mass and Earth-scale radius; ordinary thermal (ideal-gas) pressure cannot support such an object.
  • No white dwarf has ever been observed with a mass above about 1.4 M☉, and theoretical calculations independently predict exactly this mass as the maximum electron degeneracy pressure can support.

Model

  • When a Sun-like star exhausts its core hydrogen and later helium, it sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, exposing a hot, compact carbon-oxygen core: the white dwarf.
  • A white dwarf is supported against further gravitational collapse not by thermal pressure but by electron degeneracy pressure, a quantum-mechanical consequence of the Pauli exclusion principle: electrons packed to extremely high density resist further compression regardless of temperature.
  • Because degeneracy pressure has a maximum it can supply for a given mass, there is a maximum possible white dwarf mass, the Chandrasekhar limit (about 1.4 M☉); above this mass, degeneracy pressure cannot halt collapse.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \rho = \dfrac{M}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}}, \qquad M_{\rm Chandrasekhar} \approx 1.4\ M_\odot \]

Worked Example

  1. Sirius B: M = 2.025e+30 kg, R = 5.634e+06 m.
  2. Volume = (4/3)πR³ = 7.490e+20 m³.
  3. Density = M / V = 2.703e+09 kg/m³ ≈ 2.70e+06 g/cm³.
  4. For comparison, water has a density of 1,000 kg/m³ and Earth’s average density is about 5,510 kg/m³: Sirius B is roughly 2703302 times denser than water and about 490617 times denser than the entire Earth.
  5. Sirius B’s mass (1.018 M☉) sits safely below the Chandrasekhar limit (1.4 M☉), consistent with its stable, observed existence as a white dwarf rather than undergoing further collapse.

Common Misconception

Thinking a white dwarf is still generating energy by fusion. A white dwarf has no nuclear fuel left; its faint residual glow (and its slow cooling over billions of years) is purely leftover thermal energy radiating away, not ongoing energy production.

Active Learning Segment

Synthesis

A white dwarf is a stellar remnant supported by electron degeneracy pressure rather than fusion, and this quantum-mechanical support has a hard mass limit (Chandrasekhar, about 1.4 M☉) above which no white dwarf can exist.
