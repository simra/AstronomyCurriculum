The Milky Way: Structure, Rotation, and the Case for Dark Matter

Weighing a galaxy by how fast it spins

Learning Goals

  1. Describe the Milky Way’s major structural components (disk, bulge, halo).
  2. Explain how a galaxy’s rotation curve is measured and what a "flat" rotation curve implies about its mass distribution.
  3. Estimate the enclosed mass of the Milky Way at different radii from rotation-curve data.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 25.1-25.4 (the structure of the galaxy, spiral structure, the mass of the galaxy, the galactic center).

Why This Matters

The Milky Way’s rotation curve was one of the first and remains one of the most direct pieces of evidence for dark matter: if all of the Galaxy’s mass were in the visible stars and gas, rotation speeds should decline with radius outside the visible disk, the way planets slow down at greater distances from the Sun. They do not.

Opening Phenomenon

Stars and gas clouds far outside the Sun’s orbital radius (measured via 21-cm radio observations of neutral hydrogen, unaffected by dust) are observed orbiting the Galactic center at roughly the same speed (about 220 km/s) as material near the Sun’s orbit, rather than slowing down as visible starlight and gas density drop off.

First question: what here is directly observed, and what is inferred from a physical model?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • The Sun orbits the Galactic center at radius R₀ ≈ 8.2 kpc with orbital speed V₀ ≈ 220 km/s.
  • Radio observations of neutral hydrogen (21-cm emission) at radii well beyond the visible stellar disk show rotation speeds that stay roughly flat (not declining) out to at least 25 kpc.
  • The amount of visible matter (stars + gas) drops off sharply beyond about 15 kpc, yet the rotation speed does not — directly implying that most of the Galaxy’s gravitating mass at large radii is not visible.

Model

  • For a test mass orbiting in a circular orbit, Newtonian gravity gives the enclosed mass at radius R as M(R) = V(R)² R / G, where V(R) is the circular orbital speed.
  • If nearly all mass were interior to the visible disk (as for the solar system, where nearly all mass is in the Sun), V(R) should decline as 1/√R beyond the visible mass, the way planetary orbital speeds decline with distance from the Sun.
  • Because V(R) instead stays roughly flat out to large R, M(R) must keep growing linearly with R, implying an extended, largely invisible ("dark") mass distribution well beyond the visible disk.

Quantitative Tool

\[ M(R) = \dfrac{V(R)^{2} R}{G} \]

Worked Example

  1. At the Sun’s radius, R₀ = 8.2 kpc = 2.530e+20 m, V₀ = 220 km/s = 2.200e+05 m/s.
  2. M(R₀) = (2.200e+05)² × 2.530e+20 / 6.674e-11 = 1.835e+41 kg = 9.226e+10 M☉.
  3. At R = 25 kpc, V ≈ 230 km/s (still nearly flat): M(25 kpc) = 3.074e+11 M☉, roughly 3.33 times the enclosed mass at the Sun’s radius, even though the radius only increased by a factor of 3.05.
  4. The Galaxy’s visible stellar and gas mass is estimated at only about 6.0e+10 M☉ — far less than the 3.07e+11 M☉ implied by the rotation curve at 25 kpc, requiring roughly 5.1 times more mass than is visible, attributed to a dark matter halo.

Common Misconception

Assuming "dark matter" means "dark, ordinary matter we just haven’t seen yet" (like cold gas or dim stars). Multiple independent lines of evidence (rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the cosmic microwave background, large-scale structure) point to a non-baryonic form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or scatter light at all, distinguishing it from merely faint ordinary matter.

Active Learning Segment

Synthesis

A flat rotation curve, combined with Newtonian gravity, implies a mass distribution that keeps growing well beyond the visible disk — the central piece of evidence for the Milky Way’s dark matter halo.
