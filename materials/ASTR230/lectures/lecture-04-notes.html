Lecture 04: Inside a Star: Structure and Nuclear Energy Generation

ASTR 230 Stars, Galaxies, and the Universe

Context and Why This Matters

Before nuclear fusion was understood (1920s-1930s), no known energy source could power the Sun for its geologically required age of billions of years; gravitational contraction (the Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism) could only power it for tens of millions of years. Hans Bethe’s 1939 work on the proton-proton chain and CNO cycle resolved this "age of the Sun" problem and remains the foundation of stellar astrophysics.

Learning Goals

  1. State the condition of hydrostatic equilibrium and explain why stars neither collapse nor explode while on the main sequence.
  2. Describe the proton-proton chain as the Sun’s dominant energy source and compute its net energy release per reaction.
  3. Explain why higher-mass stars burn hydrogen via the CNO cycle instead.

Opening Phenomenon

The Sun fuses roughly 6 × 10¹¹ kg of hydrogen into helium every second, yet loses only about 4 × 10⁹ kg of mass per second to radiated energy (E = mc²) — a tiny fraction of its total mass, which is why it can shine steadily for billions of years.

Vocabulary

  • hydrostatic equilibrium
  • proton-proton chain
  • CNO cycle
  • mass defect
  • core temperature
  • radiative/convective transport

Evidence

  • The Sun’s central pressure and temperature (about 15 million K) are exactly what is required to balance the crushing weight of its overlying layers — confirmed by helioseismology, which measures the Sun’s internal sound-wave oscillations.
  • Solar neutrino detectors (starting with the Homestake experiment and confirmed by Super-Kamiokande and SNO) directly detect the neutrinos predicted by the proton-proton chain, confirming fusion is occurring in the Sun’s core right now.
  • More massive, hotter-cored stars are observed to have far higher CNO-cycle-dominated energy generation rates, consistent with the CNO cycle’s much steeper temperature dependence than the proton-proton chain’s.

Model

  • Hydrostatic equilibrium: at every radius inside a stable star, outward pressure from hot gas and radiation exactly balances the inward pull of gravity, so the star holds a fixed structure rather than collapsing or expanding.
  • The proton-proton chain fuses four hydrogen nuclei into one helium-4 nucleus in a sequence of two-body reactions; it dominates in stars with core temperatures below about 18 million K (roughly 1.3 solar masses and below).
  • The CNO cycle uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei as catalysts to achieve the same net fusion of four protons into helium, but its reaction rate scales far more steeply with temperature, so it dominates in hotter, more massive stars’ cores.

Working Equation

\[ 4\,{}^{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + 2e^{+} + 2\nu_e + \gamma, \qquad \Delta E = \Delta m\,c^{2} \]

Worked Example

  1. Mass of 4 hydrogen-1 nuclei: 4 × 1.007825 u = 4.031300 u.
  2. Mass of one helium-4 nucleus (plus the two positrons, which promptly annihilate and contribute their energy too): 4.002602 u.
  3. Mass defect: Δm = 4.031300 - 4.002602 = 0.028698 u, about 0.71% of the initial mass.
  4. Energy released per reaction: ΔE = Δm c² = 0.028698 × 931.494 MeV = 26.73 MeV — this is the number that, multiplied by the Sun’s reaction rate, must reproduce the Stefan-Boltzmann luminosity computed in Lecture 01, tying the nuclear-physics energy source directly to the star’s observed radiative output.

Common Misconception

Thinking a star "burns" hydrogen the way a fire burns fuel (a chemical reaction). Stellar energy generation is nuclear fusion, releasing roughly seven orders of magnitude more energy per unit mass than any chemical reaction, which is precisely why fusion (not chemistry) can power a star for billions of years.
