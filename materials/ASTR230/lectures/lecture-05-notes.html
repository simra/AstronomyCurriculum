Lecture 05: Stellar Nurseries: The Interstellar Medium and Star Formation

ASTR 230 Stars, Galaxies, and the Universe

Context and Why This Matters

Roughly 10-15% of the Milky Way’s ordinary (baryonic) matter is not locked up in stars at all, but exists as gas and dust between them. This interstellar medium is both the leftover material from earlier generations of stars and the raw material for the next generation — understanding it explains where stars come from and why star formation continues billions of years after the Galaxy formed.

Learning Goals

  1. Describe the major phases of the interstellar medium and their physical conditions.
  2. State the Jeans criterion qualitatively and explain why only the coldest, densest clouds can collapse to form stars.
  3. Trace the observable stages from molecular cloud core to protostar to zero-age main-sequence star.

Opening Phenomenon

The Orion Nebula, visible to the naked eye as a fuzzy patch in Orion’s sword, is a giant molecular cloud roughly 100 light-years across and about 200,000 solar masses of gas, actively forming thousands of new stars, some of which are directly visible as embedded protostars in infrared images.

Vocabulary

  • interstellar medium (ISM)
  • molecular cloud
  • H II region
  • Jeans mass
  • protostar
  • T Tauri star
  • zero-age main sequence

Evidence

  • Molecular clouds are cold (about 10-20 K) and dense (hundreds to thousands of molecules per cm³ compared to about 1 atom per cm³ for the ISM average) — conditions found nowhere else in interstellar space.
  • Young stellar clusters are consistently found still embedded in or immediately adjacent to molecular clouds, and never found isolated far from any cloud material, exactly as expected if clouds are the birthplace of stars.
  • Protostars show excess infrared emission from surrounding dusty disks and, in many cases, collimated bipolar jets (Herbig-Haro objects), direct observational signatures of ongoing accretion and outflow.

Model

  • A cloud can only begin gravitational collapse if its self-gravity exceeds internal pressure support, a competition captured qualitatively by the Jeans criterion: collapse is favored by high density and low temperature, and disfavored by high temperature and low density.
  • As a collapsing core’s central density and temperature rise, it becomes a protostar, radiating primarily in the infrared because it is still enshrouded in the dusty remnant of its parent cloud.
  • A protostar reaches the main sequence (becomes a genuine, hydrogen-fusing star) once its core temperature is high enough to sustain steady nuclear fusion, at which point outward radiation and gas pressure balance gravity (hydrostatic equilibrium, Lecture 04) and the star stops contracting.

Working Equation

\[ \text{collapse favored when: gravitational energy} > \text{thermal (pressure) energy} \iff M > M_{\rm Jeans} \propto T^{3/2}\rho^{-1/2} \]

Worked Example

  1. A typical Orion Nebula cloud core: T ≈ 10-20 K, n ≈ 10⁴-10⁵ molecules/cm³.
  2. By contrast, typical diffuse ISM conditions are T ≈ 100-10,000 K, n ≈ 0.1-1 atoms/cm³, roughly 10000-1000000 times less dense than a collapsing core.
  3. Because the Jeans mass falls with increasing density and decreasing temperature, only the coldest, densest cores within a molecular cloud (not the diffuse ISM as a whole, and not warmer parts of the same cloud) are unstable to collapse — explaining why star formation is localized to specific cloud cores rather than occurring everywhere at once.
  4. Once fusion ignites (Lecture 04’s energy-generation physics), the new star settles onto the main sequence and will remain there for a lifetime set by its mass, as quantified in Lecture 06 (a 3 M☉ star like those forming in Orion will spend about 642 million years on the main sequence).

Common Misconception

Picturing star formation as instantaneous or as occurring uniformly throughout interstellar space. Star formation is localized to specific, rare, dense cloud cores and unfolds over roughly 10⁵-10⁶ years from core collapse to zero-age main sequence, a blink of an eye compared to a star’s subsequent main-sequence lifetime but still far longer than any human timescale.

Active-Learning Guidance

Given two clouds with the same mass but one twice as cold and half as dense as the other, discuss qualitatively which is more likely to be collapsing, using the Jeans-criterion dependence on temperature and density.
