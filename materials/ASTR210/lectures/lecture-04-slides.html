ASTR 210 · Lecture 04

Noise, Signal-to-Noise, and Detection Limits

Detection is a probability statement

Learning Goals

  1. Write the CCD signal-to-noise equation including source, sky, dark, and read-noise terms.
  2. Compute SNR for a bright and a faint source using identical instrumental conditions.
  3. State the SNR threshold conventionally used to claim a secure detection.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 6.3 (detectors) and Chapter 17.1 (the brightness of stars).

Why This Matters

A faint source is not simply "a star you cannot quite see"; it is a source whose signal sits among several independent noise sources. Whether a signal counts as a detection is a statistical judgment, not a visual impression, and that judgment depends on the noise model, not just the raw counts.

Opening Phenomenon

Two sources produce very different counts on the same field with an identical 3×3 pixel aperture: one at 2500 electrons, one at only 300 electrons. Both sit on the same sky background and read noise, but only one is a secure detection.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

Poisson noise

Definition: Counting uncertainty that arises because independent photon arrivals fluctuate with variance approximately equal to the expected count.

Why it matters here: It establishes a fundamental noise floor even for a perfectly calibrated detector.

Relationships: For \(N\) detected electrons, \(\sigma\approx\sqrt{N}\); source, sky, and dark counting variances add in the SNR denominator.

Boundary / common confusion: Poisson noise is random scatter, not a removable calibration pattern.

sky background

Definition: Photons recorded from airglow, scattered light, unresolved sources, and other non-target illumination.

Why it matters here: Sky counts add noise inside the source aperture and can dominate a faint-source detection.

Relationships: The sky level is estimated from nearby pixels and contributes \(n_{pix}N_{sky}\) to the aperture variance.

Boundary / common confusion: Subtracting the estimated mean sky does not subtract its shot noise.

read noise

Definition: Random electronic uncertainty introduced when the detector charge is measured and digitized.

Why it matters here: It limits faint-source measurements and makes many very short exposures less efficient than fewer longer exposures.

Relationships: For an aperture of \(n_{pix}\) pixels, the variance contribution is \(n_{pix}R^2\), added in quadrature with photon-noise terms.

Boundary / common confusion: Read noise is not removed by bias subtraction; the mean bias pattern can be removed, but stochastic read noise remains.

dark current (SNR term)

Definition: The expected thermal-electron contribution per pixel over the science exposure, included as a variance source in the SNR calculation.

Why it matters here: It separates the uncertainty produced by thermal charge from the calibrated source and sky signals.

Relationships: For \(n_{pix}\) pixels, dark variance contributes \(n_{pix}N_{dark}\) when the counts are treated as Poisson.

Boundary / common confusion: This term is not the same as the residual mean after dark subtraction; the mean can be removed while its counting noise remains.

aperture

Definition: The chosen set of detector pixels whose background-subtracted counts are summed to measure a source.

Why it matters here: Aperture size controls the tradeoff between captured source flux and added sky/read noise.

Relationships: The SNR numerator grows with encircled source flux, while background and read-noise variance grow roughly with the number of included pixels.

Boundary / common confusion: A larger aperture is not automatically better and must not be confused with the telescope’s physical entrance aperture.

detection threshold

Definition: A stated signal-to-noise or false-alarm criterion used to decide whether a measured signal is distinguishable from noise.

Why it matters here: It turns “I can see something” into a reproducible statistical decision.

Relationships: A common convention is SNR near 5 for a secure isolated detection, but the false-positive rate also depends on how many locations or trials were searched.

Boundary / common confusion: A threshold is a convention tied to a noise model, not proof that the source is real under every systematic error.

shot noise

Definition: The Poisson fluctuation in the number of discrete photons or electrons counted during an exposure.

Why it matters here: It explains why relative precision improves only as the square root of the signal.

Relationships: For signal \(S\), the fractional shot-noise floor is approximately \(1/\sqrt{S}\).

Boundary / common confusion: Shot noise is not detector malfunction and cannot be calibrated away after the photons have been counted.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Repeated measurements of the same faint source scatter by an amount consistent with the square root of the total counts (Poisson statistics), not a fixed absolute error.
  • Increasing the aperture size to include more sky pixels increases the noise term even when it does not increase the captured source signal.
  • A source with SNR near 3 appears in some exposures and not others of the same field — exactly the behavior expected of a marginal detection.

Model

  • Random noise sources (source shot noise, sky shot noise, dark current shot noise, and read noise) combine in quadrature, not by simple addition.
  • SNR above about 5 is conventionally treated as a secure detection; SNR between about 3 and 5 is marginal and requires independent confirmation.
  • A quoted flux without an SNR or an aperture specification cannot be evaluated for reliability.

Quantitative Tool

\[ {\rm SNR} = \dfrac{S}{\sqrt{S + n_{\rm pix}\,(B_{\rm sky} + D + R^2)}} \]

Worked Example

  1. Aperture has n_pix = 9 pixels; sky background B = 150 e⁻/pix, dark D = 5 e⁻/pix, read noise R = 8 e⁻ (R² = 64).
  2. Noise term per pixel-summed background: n_pix × (B + D + R²) = 9 × (150 + 5 + 64) = 1971 e⁻².
  3. Bright source (S = 2500 e⁻): SNR = 2500 / √(2500 + 1971) = 2500 / 66.87 = 37.4 — a secure detection.
  4. Faint source (S = 300 e⁻): SNR = 300 / √(300 + 1971) = 300 / 47.66 = 6.3 — marginal; still above 5 but close enough that a single frame should not be over-interpreted.

Visual Reasoning

Compare the noise budgets of the bright and faint source.

  • Why does the faint source have a much lower SNR despite an identical background term?
  • What happens to each bar if the aperture (n_pix) is enlarged?

Common Pitfall

Equating "visible in the displayed image" with "securely detected." Image display stretches can make an SNR-3 fluctuation look identical to an SNR-30 source.

Active Learning Segment

Recompute the faint-source SNR after doubling the exposure time (S and background terms both double) and explain why SNR improves by less than a factor of two.

Synthesis

Detection is a probability statement built from a noise model, not merely a visual impression of the displayed image.
