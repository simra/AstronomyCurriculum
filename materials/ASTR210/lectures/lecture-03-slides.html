ASTR 210 · Lecture 03

CCD Detectors, Bias, Dark, and Flat Fields

What a modern detector actually records

Learning Goals

  1. Explain the physical origin of bias, dark current, and flat-field variations in a CCD.
  2. Derive and apply the calibration equation I_cal = (I_raw − D)/F to a real worked example, converting Lecture 1's qualitative additive/multiplicative reasoning into a computable tool.
  3. Distinguish additive corrections from multiplicative corrections.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 6.3 (visible-light detectors and instruments, CCDs).

Why This Matters

A CCD or CMOS sensor converts photons into electrons with high linearity, which is why it replaced photographic plates, but every pixel adds its own small, systematic offset and its own sensitivity. Understanding these effects at the hardware level explains why calibration frames must be taken on the same night, with the same instrument setup, as the science frames — exactly as Lecture 1’s Canon EOS M50 example did.

Opening Phenomenon

A galaxy image taken with a dirty or scratched filter shows a faint ring pattern (dust donuts) that has nothing to do with the galaxy. The same instrumental pattern appears, identically, in a flat-field exposure of blank twilight sky.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

bias frame

Definition: A nominally zero-exposure image that maps the electronic offset added during detector readout.

Why it matters here: It reveals why an unilluminated pixel can have a large positive value and establishes the additive calibration floor.

Relationships: A master bias estimates the offset and read-noise pattern; a matched dark may already contain this bias pedestal.

Boundary / common confusion: A bias frame does not measure thermal charge, and subtracting both a bias and a dark that already includes bias would double-correct the data.

dark current

Definition: Thermally generated charge accumulated in a detector pixel during an exposure even when no photons arrive.

Why it matters here: It can imitate faint source signal or produce hot pixels unless a matched dark exposure or detector model removes it.

Relationships: Dark signal generally depends on exposure time and temperature and enters the additive part of the calibration and SNR models.

Boundary / common confusion: Dark current is a signal with shot noise; a dark subtraction removes its mean pattern but cannot erase the noise already accumulated.

flat field

Definition: A normalized image of relative pixel and optical throughput measured under spatially uniform illumination.

Why it matters here: Dividing by the flat removes vignetting, dust shadows, and pixel-response variations that would otherwise bias flux comparisons.

Relationships: The flat is the multiplicative term \(F\) in \(I_{cal}=(I_{raw}-D)/F\), so values above and below one rescale counts in opposite directions.

Boundary / common confusion: A flat is valid only for the optical configuration, filter, focus, and detector state in which it was obtained.

quantum efficiency

Definition: The fraction of incident photons at a given wavelength that produce collected detector electrons.

Why it matters here: It sets wavelength-dependent sensitivity and therefore affects exposure planning and count-to-flux interpretation.

Relationships: Expected electrons scale approximately as incident photons times quantum efficiency and total optical throughput.

Boundary / common confusion: Quantum efficiency is not constant with wavelength and is not the same as the gain from electrons to ADU.

vignetting

Definition: A position-dependent loss of illumination caused by the optical system, usually strongest toward the field edges.

Why it matters here: It explains the radial flat-field pattern and why equal sources can have unequal raw counts at different detector positions.

Relationships: A normalized flat maps vignetting so division restores a common photometric response across the field.

Boundary / common confusion: Flat correction compensates stable vignetting but cannot recover photons that were never detected with the same SNR as the field center.

read noise

Definition: Random electronic uncertainty introduced when the detector charge is measured and digitized.

Why it matters here: It limits faint-source measurements and makes many very short exposures less efficient than fewer longer exposures.

Relationships: For an aperture of \(n_{pix}\) pixels, the variance contribution is \(n_{pix}R^2\), added in quadrature with photon-noise terms.

Boundary / common confusion: Read noise is not removed by bias subtraction; the mean bias pattern can be removed, but stochastic read noise remains.

linearity

Definition: The regime in which recorded counts are proportional to the number of collected electrons or incident photons.

Why it matters here: Linearity is what permits calibrated counts to be compared, averaged, and converted into relative flux.

Relationships: A linear detector obeys \(C=aN_e+b\) over its usable range, allowing additive and multiplicative calibration models.

Boundary / common confusion: Near saturation or at very low signal, detector response can depart from linearity and invalidate simple scaling.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • The Lecture 1 master bias (20 stacked frames) gives a stable mean of about 2,048 ADU with a small (1.7–2.2 ADU) scatter, independent of exposure time.
  • The matched-exposure master dark shows almost no mean-level rise above the bias (2,046 vs. 2,048 ADU) at this ISO, but a roughly 15-fold increase in pixel-to-pixel scatter, confirming that scattered hot pixels, not a uniform thermal current, dominate this camera’s dark signal at moderate exposure lengths.
  • The master flat is systematically brighter near the frame center and dimmer at the edges (vignetting from the reducer optics) and differs by a large factor between color channels because the Astronomik CLS clip filter suppresses the red channel’s throughput far more than green or blue.

Model

  • Bias and dark current are additive detector signals; when the master dark is captured at the same exposure length as the light frames, a single dark-frame subtraction removes both at once.
  • The flat field is a normalized (mean-value-one) map of relative sensitivity; dividing by it removes multiplicative structure without changing the overall brightness scale.
  • The corrected count I_cal in ADU (or electrons, once converted by the detector gain) is the quantity that should be compared across nights, instruments, and catalogs.

Quantitative Tool

\[ I_{\rm cal} = \dfrac{I_{\rm raw} - D}{F} \]

This is the equation implied by Lecture 1's additive-then-multiplicative reasoning, now stated so it can be computed directly.

Worked Example

  1. A second real patch (the "center" patch in Lab 01) records 3048 raw ADU with flat value F = 1.09.
  2. Using the same matched-exposure master dark D = 2046.0 ADU as Lecture 1: corrected signal = (3048 − 2046.0) / 1.09 = 919.3 ADU.
  3. Note that the center patch has a higher flat value (F = 1.09 > 1) than the top-left patch (F = 0.96 < 1), consistent with the vignetting pattern: the sensor center is more sensitive than its corners, so a center-patch raw count is divided up rather than down to match the true source brightness.

Visual Reasoning

Trace the vignetting profile from sensor center to edge.

  • Why is the center patch divided by a flat value above 1, and the corner patch by a value below 1?
  • What would a flat taken through a different filter do to this curve?

Common Pitfall

Using a flat field taken through a different filter, at a different focus, or on a different night than the science frames. Flats are only valid for the exact optical configuration that produced them.

Active Learning Segment

Sketch, without numbers, how a bias frame, a dark frame, and a flat frame would each look if photographed directly, and explain which corrections are additive and which are multiplicative.

Synthesis

Calibration turns raw detector counts into a defensible measurement by separating what the detector adds (bias, dark) from what it scales (flat field).
