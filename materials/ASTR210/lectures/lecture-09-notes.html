Lecture 09: Radial Velocity and Redshift

ASTR 210 Observational Astronomy and Data Reduction

Context and Why This Matters

The Doppler shift is one of the most productive tools in observational astronomy: it reveals orbital motion in binary stars, radial velocities in exoplanet detection, and cosmological redshift in distant galaxies. Its reliability depends entirely on the wavelength calibration developed in the previous lecture.

Learning Goals

  1. Apply the non-relativistic Doppler formula to measure radial velocity from a wavelength shift.
  2. Cross-check a velocity measurement using multiple independent spectral lines.
  3. Explain why line blends or calibration drift can masquerade as a velocity signal.

Opening Phenomenon

Three lines in the same spectrum — Hα, Hβ, and [O III] — are all shifted redward of their laboratory wavelengths. If the shift is a genuine bulk radial velocity, all three lines should imply the same velocity.

Vocabulary

  • rest wavelength
  • observed wavelength
  • redshift
  • blueshift
  • radial velocity
  • line blend
  • systemic velocity

Evidence

  • Hα: rest 656.3 nm, observed 656.9 nm.
  • Hβ: rest 486.1 nm, observed 486.55 nm.
  • [O III]: rest 500.7 nm, observed 501.16 nm.

Model

  • For velocities much less than the speed of light, v/c ≈ Δλ/λ₀, with positive Δλ (redshift) meaning recession.
  • A genuine bulk-motion signal produces a consistent velocity across multiple, unrelated spectral lines.
  • A velocity estimate from a single line is far less trustworthy than one confirmed by several lines of different species.

Working Equation

\[ \dfrac{v}{c} \approx \dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0} \]

Worked Example

  1. Hα: Δλ = 0.60 nm, λ₀ = 656.3 nm, v = (0.60/656.3)×299792 km/s = 274.1 km/s.
  2. Hβ: Δλ = 0.45 nm, λ₀ = 486.1 nm, v = (0.45/486.1)×299792 km/s = 277.5 km/s.
  3. [O III]: Δλ = 0.46 nm, λ₀ = 500.7 nm, v = (0.46/500.7)×299792 km/s = 275.4 km/s.
  4. All three independent lines agree to within about 1.9 km/s of the mean, v ≈ 275.7 km/s — strong evidence for a genuine, single bulk radial velocity rather than a line-specific artifact.

Common Misconception

Ascribing an observed wavelength shift to physical velocity without checking for calibration drift, line blending with a neighboring feature, or an error in identifying which transition produced the line.

Active-Learning Guidance

Given a fourth line with rest wavelength 434.0 nm, predict its observed wavelength if the source has the same radial velocity found above, then discuss what a large discrepancy from that prediction would suggest.
