ASTR 210 · Lecture 06

Differential Photometry and Light Curves

Removing common-mode effects to reveal relative variability

Learning Goals

  1. Define differential magnitude and explain why it cancels shared atmospheric and instrumental effects.
  2. Compute a light-curve dip depth from real flux ratios.
  3. Identify a poor choice of comparison star.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 17.1 (brightness and magnitudes) and Chapter 21.4 (evidence for planets from transits).

Why This Matters

Variable-star and exoplanet-transit science rarely needs an absolute magnitude; it needs a precise relative measurement between a target and a stable comparison star in the same field, observed at the same time and airmass. Differential photometry removes clouds, seeing changes, and airmass drifts that affect both stars equally.

Opening Phenomenon

A light curve of target and comparison-star fluxes over one night shows a shallow dip in the target relative to the comparison, while the comparison star alone stays flat within measurement noise.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

comparison star

Definition: A non-variable field star measured alongside a target to track transparency and instrumental changes common to both.

Why it matters here: It provides the reference needed to decide whether a target’s apparent change is intrinsic or caused by the observing system.

Relationships: The target-to-comparison flux ratio becomes a differential magnitude or normalized light curve.

Boundary / common confusion: A comparison star must be checked for stability and suitable brightness/color; sharing the field does not guarantee suitability.

differential magnitude

Definition: The logarithmic brightness difference between a target and a comparison source measured in the same frame.

Why it matters here: It suppresses common atmospheric and instrumental variations and exposes relative variability.

Relationships: \(\Delta m=-2.5\log_{10}(F_t/F_c)\); a deeper target dip increases the differential magnitude when the comparison stays stable.

Boundary / common confusion: It is a relative measurement and does not by itself supply an absolute calibrated magnitude.

common-mode noise

Definition: Variation that affects the target and reference stars similarly, such as transparency or throughput changes.

Why it matters here: Differential photometry works by canceling this shared component before interpreting target variability.

Relationships: Flux ratios or ensemble normalization remove variations correlated across many stars.

Boundary / common confusion: Color-dependent extinction, detector-position effects, or a variable comparison may not be common mode and will not cancel cleanly.

light curve

Definition: A sequence of brightness measurements plotted or modeled as a function of time.

Why it matters here: It is the primary observable used to infer variability, transit depth, event timing, and periodicity.

Relationships: Each point combines photometric measurement, timestamp, uncertainty, and often comparison-star normalization.

Boundary / common confusion: A smooth-looking curve can still be undersampled or systematically biased; cadence and error bars are part of the result.

transit depth

Definition: The fractional decrease in observed stellar flux during an occultation or transit.

Why it matters here: It quantifies the amplitude of the lecture’s dip and is the signal that must exceed photometric noise.

Relationships: For an ideal dark planet and uniform stellar disk, \(\delta\approx(R_p/R_\star)^2\), while differential magnitude gives the observed flux ratio.

Boundary / common confusion: Limb darkening, blending, starspots, and systematics make the simple radius relation approximate.

baseline

Definition: Out-of-event measurements defining the normal brightness level against which a variation is compared.

Why it matters here: A stable baseline is required to measure the depth and significance of a dip.

Relationships: Normalization often divides the light curve by a fitted baseline constructed from pre- and post-event data.

Boundary / common confusion: A short or drifting baseline can absorb real variability or create an artificial trend.

ensemble photometry

Definition: Differential photometry that combines several comparison stars into a weighted reference.

Why it matters here: It reduces reliance on any single comparison and can improve precision and robustness.

Relationships: The ensemble reference is commonly a weighted mean flux, with weights based on uncertainty and stability.

Boundary / common confusion: Adding poor, variable, saturated, or color-mismatched stars can make the ensemble worse rather than better.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • At t = 0.00 (start of the sequence): target flux = 0.995, comparison flux = 1.000 (both normalized).
  • At t = 0.10: target flux = 0.970, comparison flux = 0.999 — the comparison star barely moved while the target dropped noticeably.
  • The comparison star’s flux stays within about 0.2% of 1.000 throughout, confirming it is not itself variable.

Model

  • Differential magnitude Δm = −2.5 log10(F_target / F_comp) removes atmospheric and instrumental effects common to both stars.
  • A genuine astrophysical dip shows up as a change in the target-to-comparison ratio, not merely a change in either star’s raw flux.
  • The comparison star must be checked for its own stability before being trusted; an unstable comparison star creates a false signal in the target.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \Delta m = -2.5\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{F_{\rm target}}{F_{\rm comp}}\right) \]

Worked Example

  1. Baseline (t = 0.00): Δm = −2.5×log10(0.995/1.000) = 0.0054 mag.
  2. During the dip (t = 0.10): Δm = −2.5×log10(0.970/0.999) = 0.0320 mag.
  3. Dip depth relative to baseline: 0.0320 − (0.0054) = 0.0265 mag ≈ 27 millimagnitudes.
  4. A dip of this size (about 2.5–3% relative flux change) is comparable to a hot-Jupiter transit depth around a Sun-like star, which is why differential photometry at the millimagnitude level is central to transit science.

Visual Reasoning

Read the light curve for the target and comparison star together, not separately.

  • Why is the comparison star’s flat line essential to trusting the target’s dip?
  • What differential-magnitude value corresponds to the deepest point of the dip?

Common Pitfall

Choosing a comparison star that is itself variable, blended with a neighbor, or near saturation — any of these injects a false signal into the "constant" reference and corrupts the differential light curve.

Active Learning Segment

Given a table of target and comparison fluxes at five times, compute Δm at each time and decide whether the pattern is a real dip or measurement scatter.

Synthesis

Differential photometry reveals relative variability by removing common-mode effects that would otherwise swamp a small, real signal.
