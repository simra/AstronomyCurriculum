ASTR 210 · Lecture 09

Radial Velocity and Redshift

Velocity from calibrated wavelength shifts

Learning Goals

  1. Apply the non-relativistic Doppler formula to measure radial velocity from a wavelength shift.
  2. Cross-check a velocity measurement using multiple independent spectral lines.
  3. Explain why line blends or calibration drift can masquerade as a velocity signal.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 5.6 (the Doppler effect).

Why This Matters

The Doppler shift is one of the most productive tools in observational astronomy: it reveals orbital motion in binary stars, radial velocities in exoplanet detection, and cosmological redshift in distant galaxies. Its reliability depends entirely on the wavelength calibration developed in the previous lecture.

Opening Phenomenon

Three lines in the same spectrum — Hα, Hβ, and [O III] — are all shifted redward of their laboratory wavelengths. If the shift is a genuine bulk radial velocity, all three lines should imply the same velocity.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

rest wavelength

Definition: The laboratory or source-frame wavelength assigned to a spectral transition before Doppler shifting.

Why it matters here: It is the reference \(\lambda_0\) against which an observed displacement is converted to velocity or redshift.

Relationships: The measured shift is \(\Delta\lambda=\lambda_{obs}-\lambda_0\), entering \(v/c\approx\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0\) at low speed.

Boundary / common confusion: Air and vacuum wavelength conventions differ slightly, so the convention must match the calibration.

observed wavelength

Definition: The calibrated wavelength at which a spectral feature appears in the recorded observer-frame spectrum.

Why it matters here: It is the direct spectral measurement paired with a rest wavelength to infer motion.

Relationships: Its uncertainty combines centroiding, wavelength-solution residuals, line shape, and instrumental drift.

Boundary / common confusion: A measured feature is not automatically the intended transition; identification and blending must be checked.

redshift

Definition: A positive fractional wavelength increase \(z=(\lambda_{obs}-\lambda_0)/\lambda_0\).

Why it matters here: It quantifies the consistent displacement of multiple lines toward longer wavelengths.

Relationships: For small speeds \(v\approx cz\); at high speeds or cosmological distances the physical interpretation requires relativistic or cosmological models.

Boundary / common confusion: Redshift is an observed ratio, not always a simple peculiar velocity.

blueshift

Definition: A negative wavelength shift in which a feature is observed at shorter wavelength than its rest value.

Why it matters here: It is the sign expected for approaching radial motion in the low-speed Doppler interpretation.

Relationships: With the lecture convention, \(\Delta\lambda<0\) gives \(v<0\).

Boundary / common confusion: Calibration offsets can shift every line blueward, so sign alone does not establish physical approach.

radial velocity

Definition: The component of an object’s velocity along the observer’s line of sight.

Why it matters here: It is inferred from calibrated line shifts and is the physical quantity tested by the three-line agreement.

Relationships: At \(v\ll c\), \(v/c\approx\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0\); multiple lines should yield a common value.

Boundary / common confusion: It excludes transverse motion and may include systemic, orbital, rotational, and observer-motion contributions.

line blend

Definition: A spectral feature formed by two or more unresolved transitions whose profiles overlap.

Why it matters here: A blend can shift the apparent centroid and imitate a radial-velocity change.

Relationships: Whether components blend depends on their separation relative to the line-spread function and resolving power.

Boundary / common confusion: Treating a blended centroid as a single isolated line yields a model-dependent, potentially biased velocity.

systemic velocity

Definition: The center-of-mass line-of-sight velocity of a source system relative to a stated reference frame.

Why it matters here: It provides the common baseline around which orbital or internal line velocities vary.

Relationships: Repeated multi-line measurements can separate a stable systemic component from time-dependent motion.

Boundary / common confusion: The value depends on the adopted reference frame and is not necessarily the velocity of every emitting component.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Hα: rest 656.3 nm, observed 656.9 nm.
  • Hβ: rest 486.1 nm, observed 486.55 nm.
  • [O III]: rest 500.7 nm, observed 501.16 nm.

Model

  • For velocities much less than the speed of light, v/c ≈ Δλ/λ₀, with positive Δλ (redshift) meaning recession.
  • A genuine bulk-motion signal produces a consistent velocity across multiple, unrelated spectral lines.
  • A velocity estimate from a single line is far less trustworthy than one confirmed by several lines of different species.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \dfrac{v}{c} \approx \dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0} \]

Worked Example

  1. Hα: Δλ = 0.60 nm, λ₀ = 656.3 nm, v = (0.60/656.3)×299792 km/s = 274.1 km/s.
  2. Hβ: Δλ = 0.45 nm, λ₀ = 486.1 nm, v = (0.45/486.1)×299792 km/s = 277.5 km/s.
  3. [O III]: Δλ = 0.46 nm, λ₀ = 500.7 nm, v = (0.46/500.7)×299792 km/s = 275.4 km/s.
  4. All three independent lines agree to within about 1.9 km/s of the mean, v ≈ 275.7 km/s — strong evidence for a genuine, single bulk radial velocity rather than a line-specific artifact.

Visual Reasoning

Compare the three independent line shifts.

  • Why does agreement across Hα, Hβ, and [O III] strengthen the case for a real bulk velocity?
  • What pattern would you expect if one line were contaminated by a blend?

Common Pitfall

Ascribing an observed wavelength shift to physical velocity without checking for calibration drift, line blending with a neighboring feature, or an error in identifying which transition produced the line.

Active Learning Segment

Given a fourth line with rest wavelength 434.0 nm, predict its observed wavelength if the source has the same radial velocity found above, then discuss what a large discrepancy from that prediction would suggest.

Synthesis
