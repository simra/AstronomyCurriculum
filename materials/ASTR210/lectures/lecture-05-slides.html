ASTR 210 · Lecture 05

Aperture Photometry

Measurement design as much as calculation

Learning Goals

  1. Define instrumental magnitude and photometric zero point.
  2. Derive a zero point from one standard star and verify it against two others.
  3. Explain how aperture and sky-annulus choice bias a photometric measurement.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 17.1 (the brightness of stars, magnitudes).

Why This Matters

Aperture photometry converts pixel counts into a calibrated brightness, but the same star can yield different reported magnitudes depending on aperture radius, sky-annulus placement, and how carefully the zero point was derived. Photometry is a measurement-design problem, not only an arithmetic one.

Opening Phenomenon

A photometric pipeline processes three standard stars in the same field and filter. If the zero point derived from one star is correct, it should reproduce the catalog magnitudes of the other two without further adjustment.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

instrumental magnitude

Definition: A logarithmic measure computed directly from a background-subtracted detector count rate before absolute calibration.

Why it matters here: It places measured flux on a convenient relative scale that can be tied to catalog standards.

Relationships: The usual definition is \(m_{inst}=-2.5\log_{10}(C/t)\), and the calibrated magnitude adds a zero point and any needed color/extinction terms.

Boundary / common confusion: Instrumental magnitudes from different systems or nights are not directly comparable without calibration.

zero point

Definition: The additive constant that maps an instrumental magnitude scale onto a specified standard magnitude system.

Why it matters here: It converts count rates into calibrated magnitudes and carries calibration uncertainty into every target result.

Relationships: For a standard, \(ZP=m_{cat}+2.5\log_{10}(C/t)\); independent standards test whether the value is stable.

Boundary / common confusion: A zero point can depend on filter, atmosphere, detector, and time; it is not a permanent property of the telescope.

aperture radius

Definition: The distance from a source center defining which pixels are included in circular-aperture photometry.

Why it matters here: It controls how much of the point-spread function is captured and how much background noise enters.

Relationships: A curve of growth plots enclosed counts against radius and helps identify a radius where added source flux becomes small.

Boundary / common confusion: A radius chosen for one seeing condition or crowded field may be inappropriate for another.

sky annulus

Definition: A ring around the source aperture used to estimate the local background level per pixel.

Why it matters here: It provides the background subtraction needed to isolate source counts from sky and detector background.

Relationships: Net counts are \(C_{ap}-n_{ap}\bar C_{sky}\), so annulus contamination directly biases the measured flux.

Boundary / common confusion: The annulus must avoid source wings, neighboring stars, gradients, and bad pixels; “nearby” does not guarantee representative sky.

count rate

Definition: Background-subtracted detector counts divided by exposure time.

Why it matters here: It permits photometric comparison among frames with different exposure durations.

Relationships: Magnitude calibration uses \(C/t\) inside the logarithm rather than total counts alone.

Boundary / common confusion: Count rate corrects exposure duration, not changes in transparency, airmass, or detector response.

photometric standard

Definition: A star with a well-characterized magnitude in a defined passband, observed to calibrate an instrumental system.

Why it matters here: Standards anchor the zero point and reveal whether the night and reduction are photometrically consistent.

Relationships: Catalog magnitude and measured count rate determine the zero point; additional standards test color and extinction residuals.

Boundary / common confusion: A bright isolated star is not automatically a standard, and catalog passbands must match the measurement system.

curve of growth

Definition: The cumulative background-subtracted source counts measured as aperture radius increases.

Why it matters here: It shows where most source flux has been enclosed without adding excessive background noise.

Relationships: The plateau indicates diminishing additional flux and supports an evidence-based aperture choice.

Boundary / common confusion: In crowded fields or spatially varying backgrounds, a curve may not plateau cleanly and can include contaminating flux.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • STD-A: 150000 counts in a 30 s exposure, catalog V = 10.21.
  • STD-B: 62000 counts in 30 s, catalog V = 11.17.
  • STD-C: 24000 counts in 30 s, catalog V = 12.2.

Model

  • Instrumental magnitude is defined from a count rate (counts per second), not raw counts, so exposures of different length can be compared.
  • The zero point ZP absorbs the telescope aperture, filter throughput, and detector sensitivity into one number for a given night and setup.
  • A zero point derived from a single well-measured standard should reproduce the catalog magnitudes of independent standards if the calibration is internally consistent.

Quantitative Tool

\[ m = -2.5\log_{10}(\text{rate}) + {\rm ZP} \]

Worked Example

  1. STD-A count rate = 150000/30 = 5000.0 counts/s.
  2. Solve for the zero point using the catalog magnitude: ZP = 10.21 + 2.5×log10(5000.0) = 10.21 + 9.247 = 19.457.
  3. Apply ZP to STD-B: rate = 2066.7 counts/s, predicted m = −2.5×log10(2066.7) + 19.457 = 11.169 (catalog: 11.17) — agreement to about 1 millimagnitudes.
  4. Apply ZP to STD-C: rate = 800.0 counts/s, predicted m = −2.5×log10(800.0) + 19.457 = 12.200 (catalog: 12.2) — agreement to about 0 millimagnitudes.
  5. Because both independent checks reproduce the catalog values to a few millimagnitudes, this zero point is internally consistent and can be applied to the unknown target in the same field.

Common Pitfall

Choosing an aperture radius too small (losing source flux to the wings of the point-spread function) or too large (including excess sky noise), then blaming the resulting magnitude error on "bad seeing" rather than measurement design.
