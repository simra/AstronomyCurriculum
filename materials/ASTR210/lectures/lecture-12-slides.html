ASTR 210 · Lecture 12

Time-Domain Observing

Cadence must match the timescale of the phenomenon

Learning Goals

  1. State the Nyquist-style sampling requirement for recovering a periodic or transient signal.
  2. Evaluate whether a given cadence can resolve a specific event duration.
  3. Explain the difference between undersampling and aliasing.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 19.3 (variable stars and cosmic distances, period-luminosity work) and Chapter 21.4 (transiting exoplanets).

Why This Matters

Time-domain astronomy asks not only what an object is, but how it changes with time — and the answer depends entirely on whether the observing cadence matches the timescale of the phenomenon. A real signal can be completely invisible, or badly misrepresented, if the sampling is too coarse.

Opening Phenomenon

A transiting source produces a dip lasting about 2.1 hours. One observer images the field once per night (24-hour cadence); another images it every 20 minutes during the predicted event window.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Vocabulary for Reasoning

cadence

sampling interval

aliasing

undersampling

ingress/egress

period

duty cycle

Use these terms to describe evidence and relationships, not as isolated definitions.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • A once-per-night cadence (24 hours) is roughly 11 times coarser than the 2.1-hour event, so the event is essentially invisible in that dataset.
  • A 20-minute cadence during the event window gives roughly 6 samples across the dip, enough to resolve ingress, the flat bottom, and egress.
  • A periodic signal sampled at close to its own period can appear to be constant or slowly varying even though it is rapidly changing — classic aliasing.

Model

  • To recover the shape of an event of duration T, the sampling interval should be a small fraction of T, not merely shorter than the total observing run.
  • Sparse, evenly spaced sampling of a periodic signal can alias to a spuriously long or short apparent period.
  • Cadence, exposure time, and total baseline are three separate observing-design choices, each driven by a different aspect of the science goal.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \Delta t_{\rm cadence} \ll T_{\rm event} \]

Worked Example

  1. Event duration T = 2.1 hours.
  2. Nightly cadence Δt = 24 hours → Δt/T = 11.4, far too coarse; the event is effectively unsampled.
  3. Fine cadence Δt = 20 minutes = 0.33 hours → Δt/T = 0.159, giving about 6 samples across the event — adequate to characterize ingress, minimum, and egress.

Visual Reasoning

Compare the two sampling panels of the same event.

  • Why is the nightly-cadence panel unable to show the dip shape at all?
  • How many samples fall inside the dip in the fine-cadence panel?

Common Pitfall

Treating a sparse light curve (one point per night) as a complete time series when the phenomenon of interest varies on a timescale of hours, not days.

Active Learning Segment

Given a claimed 2.3-hour periodic signal and a proposed 6-hour cadence, decide whether the cadence is adequate and, if not, propose a better one.

Lab or Observing Connection

This unit is assessed through Problem Set 06, which pairs the stacking calculation above with a cadence-adequacy problem for the same kind of dataset.

Synthesis

Time is an observing dimension that requires deliberate cadence planning; the same phenomenon can be either well characterized or completely missed depending on sampling alone.
