ASTR 210 · Lecture 08

Spectroscopy and Wavelength Calibration

Turning a dispersed image into a scientific measurement

Learning Goals

  1. Explain why an arc-lamp exposure is required before a stellar spectrum can be interpreted.
  2. Compute spectral resolving power R = λ/Δλ and relate it to line separation.
  3. Identify which spectral features can and cannot be resolved at a given R.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 5.3 (spectroscopy in astronomy).

Why This Matters

A spectrum is only a scientific measurement once its horizontal axis has been mapped from pixel position to wavelength using a known calibration source (an arc lamp with well-documented emission lines). Without that step, a spectrum is just a picture of a rainbow.

Opening Phenomenon

A raw spectral image shows a source trace crossed by a set of bright calibration lines from a companion arc-lamp exposure. Matching those lines to a reference list is what converts "pixel column 512" into "656.3 nanometers."

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

dispersion

Definition: The change in wavelength per detector distance along the spectral direction, often expressed in nm per pixel.

Why it matters here: It converts a line’s pixel location into wavelength and sets part of the available spectral sampling.

Relationships: A wavelength solution models \(\lambda(x)\), whose derivative \(d\lambda/dx\) is the local dispersion.

Boundary / common confusion: Dispersion is not the same as resolving power; closely sampled lines may still be broadened together by the instrument profile.

arc lamp

Definition: A calibration source that produces emission lines at known laboratory wavelengths.

Why it matters here: Its line pattern supplies the pixel-wavelength correspondences used to calibrate a spectrum.

Relationships: Identified arc lines are fitted by a wavelength-solution polynomial and checked through residuals.

Boundary / common confusion: A lamp exposure can drift relative to the science spectrum if instrument conditions change between exposures.

wavelength solution

Definition: A fitted function assigning a wavelength to each spectral pixel coordinate.

Why it matters here: It turns a detector image into a spectrum from which line identities and velocities can be measured.

Relationships: The model \(\lambda(x)\) is constrained by known arc lines; fit residuals become part of the velocity uncertainty.

Boundary / common confusion: A visually good fit can still be wrong if lines are misidentified or the polynomial is extrapolated beyond calibration coverage.

resolving power

Definition: A dimensionless measure \(R=\lambda/\Delta\lambda\) of the smallest wavelength separation an instrument can distinguish near \(\lambda\).

Why it matters here: It determines whether neighboring lines can be measured separately or appear as one blended feature.

Relationships: At fixed \(R\), the resolvable interval is \(\Delta\lambda=\lambda/R\), set by optics, slit, seeing, and sampling.

Boundary / common confusion: High dispersion alone does not guarantee high resolving power if the line-spread function remains broad.

line list

Definition: A table of identified transitions and their laboratory wavelengths used for calibration or interpretation.

Why it matters here: It supplies the trusted wavelength anchors needed to fit and verify a wavelength solution.

Relationships: Observed peaks are matched to list entries, and consistency across many lines tests the identification.

Boundary / common confusion: A line list must match the lamp or species and wavelength convention; forcing an incorrect match can produce a plausible but false solution.

spectral order

Definition: One of the repeated wavelength bands produced by constructive interference in a diffraction grating, especially an echelle.

Why it matters here: Order identification is necessary because different orders can overlap spatially or in wavelength.

Relationships: The grating equation \(m\lambda=d(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta)\) links order number \(m\) to wavelength and geometry.

Boundary / common confusion: An order is not a separate physical source spectrum; it is an instrument mapping that must be extracted and merged carefully.

continuum

Definition: The smoothly varying spectral flux level on which absorption or emission lines are superposed.

Why it matters here: It defines the local reference used to measure line strength, shape, and equivalent width.

Relationships: Continuum normalization divides by a fitted baseline so line departures can be compared across wavelength.

Boundary / common confusion: In crowded or molecular spectra a true line-free continuum may be absent, making placement model-dependent.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • An arc-lamp spectrum shows several bright, sharp emission lines at well-documented laboratory wavelengths.
  • Fitting a polynomial to (pixel position, known wavelength) pairs from the arc lamp gives a wavelength solution that can be applied to the adjacent science spectrum.
  • Two spectral lines separated by less than about one resolution element blend into a single broadened feature rather than appearing as two distinct peaks.

Model

  • Resolving power R = λ/Δλ quantifies how finely a spectrograph can separate two nearby wavelengths.
  • A wavelength solution derived from arc-lamp lines must be checked for residual scatter before being trusted on science data.
  • Line identification requires both an accurate wavelength solution and a reference list of expected transitions for the object type being studied.

Quantitative Tool

\[ R = \dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} \]

Worked Example

  1. A spectrograph has a measured resolution element of Δλ = 0.3 nm near λ = 656.0 nm (the Hα region).
  2. Resolving power: R = 656.0 / 0.3 ≈ 2187.
  3. At R ≈ 2200, two lines separated by less than about 0.3 nm will blend; Hα (656.3 nm) and a hypothetical line at 656.5 nm would not be cleanly separated, but Hα and Hβ (486.1 nm), separated by over 170 nm, are trivially resolved.
  4. This matters directly for the next lecture: measuring a Doppler shift of order 0.5–0.6 nm requires a resolution element several times smaller than the shift itself, which R ≈ 2200 comfortably provides.

Common Pitfall

Measuring a line’s pixel position and reporting a "wavelength" without ever taking or applying an arc-lamp calibration exposure from the same instrumental configuration.

Active Learning Segment

Given a short table of arc-lamp pixel positions and known wavelengths, fit a straight line by hand (two-point slope) and predict the wavelength of a science-target absorption line at a specified pixel.

Lab or Observing Connection

Lab 06 (Spectroscopic Wavelength Calibration) requires deriving a wavelength solution from arc-lamp data before measuring any target line shifts.

Synthesis

Spectral calibration is what turns a dispersed image into a scientific measurement; resolving power sets the limit on what can be told apart.
