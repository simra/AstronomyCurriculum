ASTR 210 · Lecture 02

Observing Planning and Error Budgets

Turning a science goal into an executable observing plan

Learning Goals

  1. Compute airmass from altitude and estimate the resulting extinction penalty.
  2. Identify the planning decisions (target altitude, exposure time, calibration cadence) that dominate a night’s success.
  3. Distinguish a planning error from a reduction error in a failed observation.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 4.1 (Earth and sky, altitude/azimuth coordinates) and Chapter 6.1–6.2 (telescopes and observing constraints).

Why This Matters

Telescope time is scarce and expensive, and a plan built without an error budget fails quietly: the data come back, but they cannot answer the question that was asked. Professional proposals are reviewed partly on whether the requested exposure times and target altitudes are realistic.

Opening Phenomenon

A target visible at only 35° altitude looks like a fine plan on a star chart, but the light must pass through 1.74 times as much atmosphere as it would at the zenith, dimming the source by roughly 0.26 magnitudes before it even reaches the detector.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

airmass

Definition: The atmospheric path length toward a target relative to the path at the zenith.

Why it matters here: Airmass predicts how strongly extinction and atmospheric systematics affect the planned exposure.

Relationships: In the plane-parallel approximation \(X\approx\sec z\), and extinction contributes \(\Delta m=kX\).

Boundary / common confusion: The secant approximation becomes inaccurate near the horizon, where curvature and refraction matter.

zenith angle

Definition: The angular separation \(z\) between the target and the point directly overhead; it equals 90° minus altitude.

Why it matters here: It is the geometric input used to estimate airmass and therefore the target’s atmospheric penalty.

Relationships: As \(z\) grows, \(\cos z\) decreases and \(X\approx1/\cos z\) rises steeply.

Boundary / common confusion: Zenith angle is not the same as altitude; confusing them reverses the observing geometry.

extinction coefficient

Definition: The magnitude attenuation per unit airmass in a specified passband under stated atmospheric conditions.

Why it matters here: It turns target geometry into an expected loss of flux and helps decide whether an observing window is viable.

Relationships: The first-order model is \(m_{obs}=m_0+kX\), with \(k\) inferred from standards observed over a range of airmass.

Boundary / common confusion: The coefficient is not universal: wavelength, aerosols, water vapor, and time can change it.

exposure time

Definition: The interval during which a detector integrates incident photons for one recorded frame.

Why it matters here: It sets accumulated signal, saturation risk, time resolution, and part of the noise budget.

Relationships: Source and sky counts usually scale with time, while read noise is incurred once per exposure; this creates an optimization rather than a simple longer-is-better rule.

Boundary / common confusion: Longer exposure does not remove systematic errors and can blur time variability or saturate bright sources.

overhead

Definition: Observing time spent on readout, slewing, acquisition, focusing, calibration, or file handling rather than photon collection.

Why it matters here: Ignoring overhead makes a schedule impossible even when the summed exposure times fit the night.

Relationships: Efficiency is \(t_{exp}/(t_{exp}+t_{overhead})\), so short exposures can lose a large fraction of the available time.

Boundary / common confusion: Overhead is not wasted time when it is required for calibration or reliable target acquisition.

calibration cadence

Definition: The planned frequency and timing with which standards or calibration frames are obtained.

Why it matters here: It determines whether changing atmosphere and instrument response can be tracked during the science sequence.

Relationships: Calibration cadence should be shorter than the timescale on which zero point, wavelength solution, or detector state drifts.

Boundary / common confusion: One calibration at the start of a night cannot diagnose changes that occur later.

observing window

Definition: The interval when a target satisfies the project’s altitude, darkness, timing, and instrument constraints.

Why it matters here: It converts a target’s visibility into the subset of time when useful data can actually be acquired.

Relationships: The window is set by hour angle and airmass together with twilight, Moon, cadence, and required calibration time.

Boundary / common confusion: Being above the horizon is not equivalent to being scientifically observable.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Standard-star measurements repeated across an night at different altitudes show systematically fainter magnitudes at low altitude even though the star has not changed.
  • Exposure-time calculators show diminishing returns: doubling exposure time only improves signal-to-noise by about 40% because noise grows with the square root of the signal.
  • Missing a bias, dark, or flat calibration sequence cannot be repaired after the night ends, unlike a slightly short exposure.

Model

  • Airmass X approximates sec(z), where z is the zenith angle; X = 1 at the zenith and grows quickly below about 30° altitude.
  • An extinction coefficient k (mag per airmass) converts airmass into a predictable magnitude penalty, k·X.
  • A complete observing plan allocates time not only to the science target but to calibration frames, standard stars, and overheads.

Quantitative Tool

\[ X \approx \sec z = \dfrac{1}{\cos z}, \qquad \Delta m_{\rm ext} = k\,X \]

Worked Example

  1. A target is at altitude 35°, so the zenith angle is z = 90° − 35° = 55°.
  2. Airmass: X = 1/cos(55°) = 1.74.
  3. Using a typical V-band extinction coefficient k = 0.15 mag/airmass, the extra dimming is 0.15 × 1.74 = 0.26 mag relative to the zenith.
  4. A 0.26-magnitude penalty is a roughly 24% flux loss — large enough to change whether a faint target is detectable at all.

Visual Reasoning

Read the airmass curve to see why altitude choice dominates an observing plan.

  • Why does the curve steepen so quickly below 30° altitude?
  • How much flux is lost to extinction at the marked target altitude?

Common Pitfall

Choosing an exposure time based only on the target’s catalog brightness, ignoring sky brightness, airmass, and detector read noise, all of which change the achievable signal-to-noise.

Active Learning Segment

Given a target rising through the night, plot airmass versus time and identify the observing window where airmass stays below 2.0.

Lab or Observing Connection

Lab 01 opens with a short planning checklist before any calibration arithmetic begins, so a bad plan is caught before data are "collected."

Synthesis

Planning is the first stage of data reduction: a technically achievable exposure at the wrong altitude, or without calibration frames, cannot be rescued afterward.
