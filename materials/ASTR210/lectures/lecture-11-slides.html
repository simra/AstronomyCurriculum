ASTR 210 · Lecture 11

Image Stacking, Registration, and Cosmic Rays

Stacking is a statistical operation, not magic noise removal

Learning Goals

  1. Explain how signal-to-noise improves with the number of stacked, independent exposures.
  2. Compare median and mean combination for robustness to cosmic-ray hits.
  3. State the registration requirement that must be satisfied before stacking is valid.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Chapter 6.3 (detectors, CCD imaging).

Why This Matters

Deep imaging and faint-source detection depend on combining many exposures, but stacking only improves a measurement if the images are registered to a common coordinate system and the noise in each frame is independent. Careless stacking can inject artifacts rather than removing them.

Opening Phenomenon

A single 2-hour-old exposure gives SNR = 12 for a faint source — barely a secure detection. Combining 9 such exposures, taken under similar conditions, should improve the detection significantly if the images are well registered.

First question: what here is directly measured, and what is inferred from a model?

Terms for This Lecture

image registration

Definition: The transformation and resampling that align multiple images to a common pixel or sky-coordinate grid.

Why it matters here: Registration ensures that real source signal lands at the same output location before frames are combined.

Relationships: Centroids or WCS solutions constrain shifts, rotation, scale, and sometimes distortion.

Boundary / common confusion: Registration after stacking cannot undo source smearing already introduced by misalignment.

median combine

Definition: A stack in which each output pixel is the median of the aligned input-pixel values.

Why it matters here: It rejects isolated cosmic-ray values without requiring their amplitudes to be modeled.

Relationships: For an odd number of frames, one extreme outlier does not change the central ordered value.

Boundary / common confusion: The median is robust but statistically less efficient than the mean for purely Gaussian noise and small stacks.

sigma-clipping

Definition: An iterative combination method that estimates a center and scatter, rejects values beyond a chosen sigma threshold, and recomputes the result.

Why it matters here: It retains much of the mean’s efficiency while rejecting cosmic rays and other outliers.

Relationships: The threshold is defined relative to a noise estimate and therefore depends on stack depth and the validity of that estimate.

Boundary / common confusion: Aggressive clipping can remove real variable or moving-source signal, especially with few frames.

cosmic ray

Definition: An energetic-particle event that deposits charge in one exposure, often as a sharp bright pixel or track.

Why it matters here: Its single-frame nature distinguishes it from a stationary astronomical source and motivates robust stacking.

Relationships: Registered stacks expose cosmic rays as temporal outliers at a pixel while persistent source flux repeats.

Boundary / common confusion: Not every isolated bright pixel is a cosmic ray; hot pixels, satellites, and transients require different checks.

independent noise

Definition: Random fluctuations whose values in one exposure do not predict their values in another.

Why it matters here: The square-root stacking gain assumes this independence across frames.

Relationships: Independent variances add, so averaging \(N\) comparable frames reduces standard error by \(1/\sqrt N\).

Boundary / common confusion: Flat-field errors, sky gradients, and guiding patterns repeated across frames are correlated systematics and do not average down this way.

stack depth

Definition: The number of usable aligned exposures, or equivalently the total integrated exposure represented by a stack.

Why it matters here: It sets the expected random-noise improvement and the robustness available for outlier rejection.

Relationships: For equal independent frames, SNR grows approximately as \(\sqrt N\); total exposure grows as \(Nt\).

Boundary / common confusion: Nominal frame count overstates effective depth if frames differ greatly in quality or share correlated errors.

outlier rejection

Definition: A documented rule for identifying and excluding measurements inconsistent with the expected distribution or temporal behavior.

Why it matters here: It prevents cosmic rays and defective pixels from dominating combined images.

Relationships: Median combination and sigma-clipping are two rejection strategies with different efficiency and sample-size requirements.

Boundary / common confusion: Rejecting points solely because they disagree with the desired result is not valid outlier treatment.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • A cosmic-ray strike appears as a single, very bright pixel in exactly one frame of a stack and nowhere else — it is not reproduced across exposures the way real astrophysical signal is.
  • Stacking without first aligning (registering) the frames to a common pixel grid blurs point sources and can create false extended structure.
  • A simple mean combine is pulled toward the cosmic-ray value in the affected pixel; a median combine of nine frames is essentially unaffected by one outlier.

Model

  • If per-frame noise is independent and Gaussian, stacking N frames improves SNR by √N relative to a single frame.
  • Median combination (or sigma-clipped mean) is far more robust to single-frame outliers like cosmic rays than a simple arithmetic mean.
  • Registration (aligning frames to a common World Coordinate System or pixel grid) must happen before combination, not after.

Quantitative Tool

\[ {\rm SNR}_{\rm stack} \approx \sqrt{N}\,\times\,{\rm SNR}_{\rm single} \]

Worked Example

  1. Single-frame SNR = 12; stacking N = 9 independent, registered frames gives an expected improvement factor of √9 = 3.00.
  2. Stacked SNR ≈ 12 × 3.00 = 36.0 — a marginal detection becomes a secure one.
  3. If one of the nine frames contains a 5000-count cosmic-ray spike in the target pixel, a simple mean combine adds roughly 5000/9 ≈ 556 counts of spurious signal to that pixel, while a median combine of nine values is essentially untouched by the single spike.

Visual Reasoning

Read the stacking-gain curve alongside the cosmic-ray comparison.

  • Why does the SNR gain grow more slowly as N increases?
  • Why does a median combine resist the single spiked frame while a mean combine does not?

Common Pitfall

Assuming that stacking automatically removes artifacts. Stacking only removes random, independent noise; it does not remove a systematic error (like an uncorrected flat field) present in every frame, and a naive mean combine does not remove cosmic rays.

Active Learning Segment

Given nine simulated per-frame values for one pixel, one of which is a clear outlier, compute both the mean and median and discuss which combination method a careful reduction pipeline should use.

Lab or Observing Connection

This unit is assessed through Problem Set 06 rather than a dedicated lab, using simulated per-frame values that mirror this worked example.

Synthesis

Stacking is a statistical operation that trades exposure count for signal-to-noise; it rewards careful registration and is easily defeated by sloppy combination methods.
