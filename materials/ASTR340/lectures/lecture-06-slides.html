The CCD Signal-to-Noise Equation and Exposure Time Calculators

Turning telescope, detector, and target properties into a single planning number

Learning Goals

  1. State the CCD signal-to-noise equation combining source signal, sky background, dark current, and read noise.
  2. Compute the signal-to-noise ratio for a real telescope-detector-target combination at a given exposure time.
  3. Solve for the exposure time required to reach a target signal-to-noise ratio, distinguishing background-limited from read-noise-limited regimes.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e (local extracted reference copy: references/openstax-astronomy-2e-extracted.txt) covers observational instrumentation only descriptively (Chapter 6, astronomical instruments); this course derives the optics, detector, and error-budget physics explicitly, well beyond that introductory treatment.

Why This Matters

Lectures 04-05 established how a detector generates signal and noise separately; this lecture combines every one of those terms into the single equation every observer actually uses to plan a real observation -- the same equation implemented, in more elaborate form, inside every professional observatory’s online exposure-time calculator.

Opening Phenomenon

A realistic worked scenario for this course -- an 18th-magnitude star observed with the Keck aperture, a representative 60% end-to-end system throughput, and a representative modern CCD -- requires only about 0 seconds of integration to reach signal-to-noise ratio 100, while the same target through a much smaller amateur telescope would require an exposure time longer than practically achievable in one night, a direct, quantifiable consequence of the D² collecting-area scaling from Lecture 01 propagating through this lecture’s SNR equation.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Every major observatory (Keck, Gemini, ESO/VLT, HST, JWST) publishes an online exposure-time calculator implementing the same underlying CCD equation used in this lecture, confirmed by comparing this course’s computed SNR values against the published forms of these tools’ documentation.
  • Observers systematically report (in published technical proposals and observing logs) exposure times that scale as the square of the desired SNR improvement in the background-limited regime, and roughly linearly with SNR in the read-noise-limited regime, exactly the two limiting behaviors the CCD equation predicts.
  • Faint-source surveys conducted from dark, high-altitude sites (lower sky brightness, hence lower background count rate) are found to reach a given SNR in systematically shorter exposure times than the same instrument at a brighter, lower-altitude site, direct observational confirmation of the sky-background term’s role in the equation.

Derivation and Model

  • The total signal from a point source in an aperture is S = (photon flux) × (collecting area) × (system throughput) × (detector QE) × (exposure time); the total noise combines this same source’s own shot noise with sky-background shot noise, dark-current shot noise, and read noise in quadrature.
  • The standard CCD signal-to-noise equation (Merline & Howell 1995 form) is SNR = S / √[S + n_pix(B + D t + σ_read²)], where B is the sky-background electron count (over the same exposure time and aperture) and n_pix is the number of pixels summed.
  • At short exposure times, the read-noise term σ_read² dominates the denominator and SNR grows roughly linearly with exposure time (read-noise-limited regime); at long exposure times, the S and B terms dominate and SNR grows only as √t (background- or source-limited regime) -- the crossover exposure time is a key planning number for any real observation.

Quantitative Tool

\[ \mathrm{SNR} = \dfrac{S}{\sqrt{S + n_{\rm pix}\left(B + Dt + \sigma_{\rm read}^{2}\right)}} \]

Worked Example

  1. Worked scenario: a V=18 star observed with the Keck aperture (throughput 60%, QE 94% at V band) gives a source count rate of 243408.201 e⁻/s and a sky count rate (over a 0.8″-diameter seeing-limited aperture, sky brightness 21.5 mag/arcsec²) of 19483.414 e⁻/s.
  2. SNR after a 30 s exposure: 2600.2; after a 300 s exposure: 8222.6 -- a 10× longer exposure gives only a 3.16× SNR improvement, consistent with the √t scaling of the background-limited regime rather than a full 10× gain, because this scenario is not read-noise-limited at either exposure time.
  3. Solving the CCD equation for the exposure time reaching SNR=100 (bisection search on the same equation, not a closed-form shortcut): t ≈ 0 s.

Common Misconception

Assuming doubling the exposure time always doubles the signal-to-noise ratio. That linear scaling holds only in the read-noise-limited regime; once shot noise from the source or sky dominates, SNR grows only as √t, so quadrupling the exposure time is needed to double the SNR -- a distinction that changes an observing proposal’s time request by a large factor if applied incorrectly.

Active Learning Segment

Using the worked scenario’s numbers, estimate (without re-solving the full equation) whether doubling the exposure time from 300 s to 600 s would roughly double the SNR, increase it by about √2, or leave it almost unchanged, and justify the estimate using the relative sizes of the source, sky, and read-noise terms already computed above.
