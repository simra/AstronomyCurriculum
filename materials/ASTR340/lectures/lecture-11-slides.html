Adaptive Optics: Wavefront Sensing and the Strehl Ratio

Undoing the atmosphere in real time

Learning Goals

  1. Describe the physical origin of atmospheric wavefront distortion and the basic adaptive-optics correction loop.
  2. Derive the Maréchal approximation relating residual wavefront error to Strehl ratio.
  3. Interpret real Keck adaptive optics performance data using the Strehl ratio and connect it to the diffraction limit of Lecture 02.

Why This Matters

Lecture 02 showed that ground-based telescopes rarely reach their diffraction limit because of atmospheric seeing. Adaptive optics is the engineered solution to exactly that problem: measuring and correcting atmospheric wavefront distortion fast enough to recover most of a large telescope’s theoretical resolution, closing the gap this course’s second lecture first identified.

Opening Phenomenon

Keck’s adaptive optics system, using a laser guide star to create an artificial reference beacon and a deformable mirror updated hundreds of times per second, can turn Keck’s seeing-limited image (roughly 58× coarser than its diffraction limit, Lecture 02) into a near-diffraction-limited image with a Strehl ratio as high as 0.81 in K band -- recovering most, though not all, of the sharpness a 10 m aperture is physically capable of delivering.

First question: what here is directly observed or a published instrument specification, and what follows only once a physical law is derived and applied?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Real-time wavefront sensors (typically Shack-Hartmann sensors) at observatories including Keck are documented to measure atmospheric wavefront distortion using a bright natural star or an artificial laser guide star, and feed a correction signal to a deformable mirror updated at rates of hundreds to over a thousand times per second, fast enough to track atmospheric turbulence that itself changes on millisecond timescales.
  • Measured Strehl ratios for operating adaptive-optics systems are found to depend strongly on both observing wavelength (higher Strehl at longer wavelengths, for the same residual wavefront error) and atmospheric conditions (higher Strehl in better seeing), exactly the wavelength- and σ-dependence the Maréchal approximation predicts.
  • AO-corrected images from Keck and similar systems are documented (via direct point-spread-function measurements of stars) to approach, but not fully reach, the diffraction-limited Airy pattern of Lecture 02, with a residual "seeing halo" surrounding a sharper diffraction-limited core -- direct observational confirmation that AO correction is real but imperfect, exactly as a Strehl ratio below 1.0 implies.

Derivation and Model

  • Atmospheric turbulence introduces a spatially and temporally varying optical path-length delay across a telescope’s aperture, distorting an initially flat incoming wavefront; a wavefront sensor measures this distortion (often using light from a bright star, or an artificial laser-excited sodium-layer beacon when no sufficiently bright natural star lies near the target) many times per second.
  • A deformable mirror, its surface shape controlled by dozens to thousands of actuators, is driven in a closed feedback loop to apply the equal-and-opposite correction to the wavefront, flattening it before it reaches the science instrument; residual, uncorrected wavefront error (rms σ, typically 100-300 nm for a well-performing natural-guide-star system) remains because of finite actuator count, finite correction speed, and imperfect wavefront sensing.
  • The Maréchal approximation relates this residual rms wavefront error directly to the Strehl ratio, S = exp[-(2πσ/λ)²], the ratio of the achieved peak image intensity to the theoretical diffraction-limited peak intensity; S=1 corresponds to a perfect, fully diffraction-limited correction, and S approaching 0 corresponds to an essentially uncorrected, seeing-limited image.

Quantitative Tool

\[ S = \exp\left[-\left(\dfrac{2\pi\sigma}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\right], \qquad \mathrm{FWHM}_{\rm diff} \approx 1.03\,\dfrac{\lambda}{D} \]

Worked Example

  1. Representative Keck natural-guide-star AO residual wavefront error, σ=160 nm (Wizinowich et al. 2000, standard published value), evaluated at K band (λ=2200 nm): S = exp[-(2π×160/2200)²] = 0.812.
  2. The same residual wavefront error evaluated in V band instead of K band would give a Strehl ratio of 0.0358 -- essentially zero correction -- which is exactly why natural-guide-star adaptive optics historically works far better in the near-infrared than the visible for the same physical correction hardware.
  3. Keck’s diffraction-limited K-band FWHM: 46.7 milliarcsec (from FWHM≈1.03λ/D); at Strehl 0.81, the AO-corrected image core approaches, but does not exactly match, this diffraction-limited width, with the remainder of the light spread into a broader residual halo.

Common Misconception

Treating adaptive optics as a complete fix for atmospheric blurring. Even excellent natural-guide-star AO rarely exceeds Strehl ratios of 0.3-0.6 in the near-infrared under good conditions, and performs far worse in the visible (as this lecture’s worked example shows quantitatively); AO substantially improves, but does not eliminate, the gap between seeing-limited and truly diffraction-limited imaging identified in Lecture 02.

Synthesis

Adaptive optics measures and corrects atmospheric wavefront distortion in real time, and the Maréchal approximation’s Strehl ratio quantifies exactly how close that correction brings a real system to the diffraction limit Lecture 02 established as the theoretical ceiling -- a ceiling AO approaches, especially in the infrared, but essentially never reaches in the visible with current natural-guide-star technology.
