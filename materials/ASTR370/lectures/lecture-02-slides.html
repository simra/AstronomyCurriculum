The Solar Atmosphere: Photosphere, Chromosphere, and the Corona

The coronal heating problem: why the outer atmosphere is hotter than the surface below it

Learning Goals

  1. Describe the photosphere, chromosphere, transition region, and corona and their real characteristic temperatures.
  2. Explain why a naive expectation of monotonically falling temperature with height fails dramatically above the photosphere.
  3. Connect the coronal-heating problem to the same magnetic processes (Unit 2 onward) responsible for the Sun’s activity.

Why This Matters

Lecture 01’s hydrostatic/scale-height framework assumed a fixed temperature; this lecture confronts the observed temperature structure of the real solar atmosphere, which rises sharply above the photosphere rather than continuing to fall -- one of solar physics’ oldest unsolved problems, and the direct motivation for this course’s later magnetic-activity units.

Opening Phenomenon

Moving outward from the photosphere (T_eff=5772 K), the temperature first falls to a minimum of roughly 4100 K, then rises through the chromosphere to about 2e+04 K, and then jumps abruptly, across a transition region only a few hundred kilometers thick, to the corona’s million-plus-kelvin temperature (1.5e+06 K) -- a temperature increase of more than two orders of magnitude in a region with no additional nearby heat source as obvious as the solar interior below the photosphere.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • The corona is directly visible during total solar eclipses (and continuously via coronagraphs, Unit 5) as an extended, faint, structured outer atmosphere, and its emission-line spectrum (highly ionized iron lines such as Fe XIV, requiring temperatures of order 10^6 K to produce those ionization states) was the key 20th-century evidence establishing its extreme temperature.
  • The chromosphere, visible briefly as a pink flash at the start/end of a total eclipse (dominated by hydrogen-alpha emission), sits at intermediate temperatures between the photosphere and corona but still well below the corona’s temperature.
  • Parker Solar Probe (Unit 5) and other missions have directly measured Alfven-wave activity and small-scale magnetic reconnection events ("nanoflares," a mechanism proposed by Eugene Parker, the mission’s namesake) in the near-Sun corona, both leading candidate mechanisms for solving the coronal heating problem.

Derivation and Model

  • If the solar atmosphere were in pure radiative equilibrium with no additional energy input, temperature would fall monotonically outward (as it does through the photosphere and into the temperature minimum); the chromospheric rise and especially the coronal jump require a non-radiative heating mechanism that deposits mechanical or magnetic energy directly into the outer atmosphere.
  • The two leading physical mechanisms are (1) Alfven-wave heating, in which magnetohydrodynamic waves generated by convective motions below the photosphere propagate upward and dissipate their energy in the corona, and (2) nanoflare heating, in which numerous small-scale magnetic reconnection events (too small individually to resolve as flares, Unit 3) collectively deposit enough energy to sustain coronal temperatures.
  • Both mechanisms require the Sun’s magnetic field as the essential energy-transport agent (rather than photons or bulk convective flow directly), directly connecting this lecture’s open problem to Unit 2’s treatment of the solar magnetic field and Unit 3’s flare energetics.

Quantitative Tool

\[ T_{\rm corona} \gg T_{\rm chromosphere} > T_{\rm eff} \ \text{despite increasing distance from the nuclear energy source (requires non-radiative heating)} \]

Worked Example

  1. Using Lecture 01’s scale-height formula at the coronal temperature and mean molecular weight gives H_corona=75,260 km, compared to the photospheric H=134 km -- the corona’s huge scale height (a ratio of 563) is why it extends visibly for millions of kilometers rather than remaining a thin surface layer like the photosphere.
  2. The coronal base electron density (n~1e+08 cm^-3, a standard published quiet-Sun value) is many orders of magnitude lower than the photospheric density, so despite its enormous temperature, the corona’s total thermal energy content per unit volume is modest -- a genuine, quantifiable reason the corona is not a bright thermal-emission source at optical wavelengths despite its temperature.

Common Misconception

Assuming higher temperature always means more visually or energetically dominant. The corona’s temperature vastly exceeds the photosphere’s, yet the photosphere emits far more visible light per unit area (Stefan-Boltzmann, proportional to T^4, is not directly applicable to the extremely tenuous corona, which is optically thin and not a blackbody emitter) -- do not conflate "hotter" with "brighter in visible light."

Synthesis

The unsolved coronal heating problem -- an outer atmosphere far hotter than the surface beneath it -- points directly to the solar magnetic field as the missing energy-transport agent, motivating this course’s next unit on solar magnetism and the sunspot cycle.
