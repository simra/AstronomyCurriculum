ASTR 420 Lecture 13: The Matter Power Spectrum

Cosmology · study notes

Learning objectives

  • Read a power spectrum as a record of primordial perturbations and later processing.
  • Explain Fourier modes, turnover, and baryon features using the governing relation.
  • Separate measurement, calibration, inference, and model limitation.

Lecture arc

A power spectrum is a statistical summary; the same broad shape can be affected by bias, redshift space, and nonlinear evolution. Begin with the observational puzzle and ask students what is directly measured. Introduce Fourier modes, turnover, and baryon features only after the data-model boundary is explicit.

The visual is a reasoning object: students should narrate what each line, region, or axis means before seeing the equation.

Derivation and interpretation

\(\\langle\\delta_{\\bf k}\\delta^*_{\\bf k'}\\rangle=(2\\pi)^3P(k)\\delta^3(\\bf k-\\bf k')\)

Define the symbols, units, and assumptions. Explain why the relation is useful and where it fails. Relate the result to the paired lab's data record rather than treating the equation as a detached recipe.

Worked example

The supplied power table has P(0.10)/P(0.01) = 5.

The ratio is not a primordial amplitude alone; transfer, bias, and nonlinear effects shape the observed spectrum. Require students to estimate the answer, show units, and state whether the approximation is controlled, empirical, or merely illustrative.

Misconceptions and study guidance

A power spectrum is a statistical summary; the same broad shape can be affected by bias, redshift space, and nonlinear evolution. Common traps include confusing redshift with a unique velocity, treating a model-dependent parameter as a direct measurement, and overlooking covariance. Study by reproducing the calculation, changing one assumption, and comparing the result with the exact OpenStax anchor listed in the reference log.

Preparation for the problem set: write a one-paragraph evidence chain from observation to conclusion and identify the weakest link.
