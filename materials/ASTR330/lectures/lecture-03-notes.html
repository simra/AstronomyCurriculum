Lecture 03: Radiative Energy Transport: How Heat Actually Gets Out

ASTR 330 Astrophysics II: Stellar Structure and Evolution

Context and Why This Matters

ASTR 310 derived the radiative transfer equation for a beam of light crossing a slab of gas. This lecture applies the same optical-depth formalism to a star’s interior, where photons undergo a random walk of enormous numbers of absorption/re-emission and scattering events, and shows how that random walk sets the star’s temperature gradient -- and, ultimately, its surface luminosity.

Learning Goals

  1. Derive the radiative diffusion approximation for energy transport from the radiative transfer equation (ASTR 310, Lecture 03).
  2. Derive the dimensionless radiative temperature gradient ∇_rad and identify the physical quantities that control it.
  3. Evaluate ∇_rad for real solar-interior conditions and compare it across two very different depths.

Opening Phenomenon

A photon born in the Sun’s core takes on the order of tens of thousands of years to random-walk its way to the surface, despite light itself crossing the Sun’s radius in about 2.3 seconds -- the discrepancy is entirely due to the enormous number of absorption and re-emission events (set by the core’s huge optical depth) the photon undergoes along the way.

Vocabulary

  • radiative diffusion
  • mean free path
  • radiative flux
  • dimensionless temperature gradient ∇_rad
  • radiation constant
  • photon random walk

Evidence

  • In the high-optical-depth interior of any star, the local radiation field is very nearly isotropic and close to a blackbody at the local temperature, exactly the condition under which the diffusion approximation to radiative transfer (rather than the full transfer equation) is valid.
  • Helioseismic inversions of the Sun’s internal sound-speed and temperature profile are consistent with a standard solar model built by integrating the radiative-diffusion energy-transport equation through the radiative zone (below about 0.71 R☉) and a convective prescription above it (Lecture 04), not with a single transport law applied throughout.
  • More opaque or more luminous stellar interiors are found (in numerical stellar models) to require a systematically steeper temperature gradient to carry the same energy flux, exactly matching the radiative-diffusion equation’s direct proportionality between ∇_rad and both κ and L.

Derivation and Model

  • In the diffusion limit (optical depth ≫ 1), the radiative flux is F = -(4ac T³)/(3κρ) dT/dr, a direct analog of Fourier heat conduction with an effective "radiative conductivity" set by the opacity.
  • Multiplying F by the local surface area 4πr² gives the local luminosity, L(r) = -16πr² (acT³)/(3κρ) dT/dr; solving this for dT/dr and non-dimensionalizing using hydrostatic equilibrium (dP/dr = -GMρ/r²) gives the standard dimensionless radiative gradient ∇_rad = (d ln T/d ln P)_rad = 3κ L P /(16π a c G M T⁴).
  • ∇_rad depends directly on the local opacity κ (Lecture 02) and luminosity L, and inversely on the enclosed mass M and T⁴; a region with unusually high opacity or unusually high local luminosity-to-mass ratio requires an unusually steep temperature gradient to carry that luminosity radiatively -- and, as Lecture 04 shows, a gradient that becomes too steep triggers convective instability instead.

Working Equation

\[ F = -\dfrac{4acT^{3}}{3\kappa\rho}\dfrac{dT}{dr}, \qquad \nabla_{\rm rad} \equiv \left(\dfrac{d\ln T}{d\ln P}\right)_{\rm rad} = \dfrac{3\kappa L P}{16\pi a c G M T^{4}} \]

Worked Example

  1. Deep solar envelope sample point (r ≈ 0.7 R☉, T ≈ 2.0e+06 K, P ≈ 6.0e+13 Pa, electron-scattering opacity from Lecture 02): ∇_rad = 3κLP/(16πacGMT⁴) = 0.0982.
  2. Near-surface sample point (T ≈ 5.0e+04 K, P ≈ 2.0e+10 Pa, ρ ≈ 1.0e-03 kg/m³, schematic Kramers opacity κ ≈ 153.84 m²/kg from the much lower temperature and higher bound-free/H⁻ opacity there): ∇_rad = 3.739e+05.
  3. The deep-envelope ∇_rad (0.098) is far smaller than the near-surface ∇_rad (3.739e+05), driven almost entirely by the much larger near-surface opacity from Lecture 02’s Kramers scaling at low T -- Lecture 04 shows precisely which of these two numbers exceeds the adiabatic gradient and therefore triggers convection.

Common Misconception

Assuming the diffusion approximation applies everywhere in a star, including near the photosphere. The diffusion approximation strictly requires optical depth ≫ 1; near a star’s surface (optical depth of order unity, ASTR 310 Lecture 03), the full radiative transfer equation, not the diffusion limit, must be used, which is why stellar-atmosphere modeling and stellar-interior modeling are treated as separate problems joined at a boundary.
