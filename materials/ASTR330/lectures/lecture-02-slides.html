ASTR 330 · Lecture 02

Equation of State and Opacity: What a Photon Fights Against

Closing the system with two more physical ingredients

Learning Goals

  1. State the ideal-gas-plus-radiation equation of state and identify the regimes where each pressure term dominates.
  2. Identify the three principal microscopic opacity sources in stellar interiors (electron scattering, free-free, bound-free) and their approximate temperature/density scaling.
  3. Compute and compare electron-scattering and Kramers-type opacity estimates for real solar-interior conditions.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e does not develop the equation of state or opacity formalism explicitly; this course derives both from first principles, connecting to the qualitative discussion of the Sun’s interior in Chapter 16.2.

Why This Matters

The hydrostatic-equilibrium equation relates P, M, and ρ, but leaves P, ρ, and T related only by an unspecified equation of state; the energy-transport equation (Lecture 03) requires an opacity κ(ρ,T) as an input. Both of these missing physical ingredients are supplied here, finally making the four-equation system of Lecture 01 solvable in principle.

Opening Phenomenon

The Sun’s core, with an estimated central density around 1.5e+05 kg/m³ -- denser than solid lead -- is nonetheless accurately described by the ideal-gas law, because at a core temperature of 1.57e+07 K, thermal energies vastly exceed the quantum degeneracy energy scale that would otherwise invalidate that assumption (a limit explored quantitatively in Lecture 12 for white dwarfs).

First question: what here is directly observed, and what follows only once a physical law is derived and applied?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • The ratio of radiation pressure to gas pressure, negligible for the Sun (as found quantitatively in ASTR 310, Lecture 13’s Eddington-luminosity discussion), becomes progressively more important for higher-mass main-sequence stars, exactly as the P_rad ∝ T⁴ versus P_gas ∝ ρT scaling predicts.
  • Stellar opacity is observed (via detailed spectral and solar/asteroseismic modeling) to fall steeply with increasing temperature in the deep stellar interior, consistent with the Kramers free-free/bound-free opacity law’s T⁻³·⁵ scaling, before flattening at the highest temperatures where electron scattering (temperature-independent) takes over.
  • Numerically tabulated OPAL/OP opacities, used in all modern stellar-evolution codes, show a pronounced peak near 10⁵-10⁶ K from iron-group bound-bound/bound-free transitions (the "iron opacity bump"), a feature entirely absent from the simple Kramers or electron-scattering scalings used for order-of-magnitude estimates in this lecture.

Derivation and Model

  • The total pressure in a stellar interior is the sum of ideal-gas pressure and radiation pressure, P = (ρkT)/(μm_p) + (1/3)aT⁴, with a = 4σ/c the radiation constant; which term dominates depends on the local ρ and T, with radiation pressure favored at high T and low ρ.
  • Electron-scattering (Thomson) opacity is independent of temperature and density (for non-relativistic, fully ionized gas): κ_es = σ_T(1+X)/(2m_p), where X is the hydrogen mass fraction; it dominates in hot, fully ionized stellar interiors where bound electrons are unavailable for bound-free absorption.
  • Free-free and bound-free opacity together are often approximated by the Kramers opacity law, κ_Kramers ∝ ρ T⁻³·⁵, a steep temperature dependence that makes this opacity source dominant in cooler, denser stellar envelopes and negligible in the hottest stellar cores, where electron scattering instead sets the floor.

Quantitative Tool

\[ P = \dfrac{\rho k T}{\mu m_p} + \dfrac{1}{3}aT^{4}, \qquad \kappa_{\rm es} = \dfrac{\sigma_T(1+X)}{2m_p}, \qquad \kappa_{\rm Kramers} \propto \rho T^{-3.5} \]

Worked Example

  1. Electron-scattering opacity for a solar-composition (X=0.70) fully ionized gas: κ_es = σ_T(1+X)/(2m_p) = 0.0338 m²/kg (equivalently 0.338 cm²/g), independent of the local temperature or density.
  2. Schematic Kramers-law opacity at the Sun’s core conditions (ρ ≈ 1.5e+05 kg/m³, T ≈ 1.57e+07 K, illustrative coefficient not a precision OPAL value): κ_Kramers ≈ 42.0640 m²/kg -- about 1244 times larger than κ_es at these conditions. This large a discrepancy is a genuine limitation of the illustrative Kramers coefficient used here (chosen for its correct T⁻³·⁵ functional form, not calibrated to reproduce a precise core value): real standard-solar-model opacity tables (OPAL/OP) find electron scattering and free-free absorption contribute comparably in the Sun’s deep core, so this schematic estimate should be read as demonstrating the correct qualitative T⁻³·⁵ scaling, not as a quantitatively accurate core opacity.
  3. Radiation-to-gas pressure ratio at the Sun’s core: P_rad/P_gas = (1/3)aT⁴ / (ρkT/μm_p) with μ ≈ 0.62 (fully ionized solar composition); evaluating at T = 1.57e+07 K and ρ = 1.5e+05 kg/m³ gives P_rad/P_gas ≈ 4.89e-04, confirming radiation pressure is negligible in the Sun’s core, consistent with ASTR 310’s independent finding (Lecture 13) that the Sun operates far below its own Eddington luminosity.

Common Misconception

Assuming a single opacity law (Kramers, or electron scattering alone) applies throughout an entire star. Real stellar opacity is a composite of several mechanisms whose relative importance shifts with local ρ and T; using Kramers scaling in a fully ionized, radiation-pressure-dominated massive-star core, or using pure electron scattering in a cool, partially ionized envelope, both give qualitatively wrong answers.

Synthesis

The equation of state and the opacity law are the two missing physical ingredients that, together with mass continuity and hydrostatic equilibrium (Lecture 01), the energy-conservation equation (Lectures 05-06), and the energy-transport equation (Lecture 03), complete the full system of stellar-structure equations.
