Lecture 07: The Mass-Luminosity Relation and Main-Sequence Lifetimes

ASTR 330 Astrophysics II: Stellar Structure and Evolution

Context and Why This Matters

Every real, precisely measured star used in this course (Alpha Centauri A and B, Sirius A, and the Sun) can now be placed on a single, quantitative mass-luminosity-lifetime framework, which is also the essential tool (developed further in Lecture 09) for dating star clusters from their main-sequence turnoff.

Learning Goals

  1. Derive the approximate mass-luminosity scaling L ∝ M^3.5 from homology-style reasoning connecting core temperature, opacity, and hydrostatic equilibrium.
  2. Derive the main-sequence lifetime scaling t_MS ∝ M/L ∝ M^-2.5 from the fuel-supply argument.
  3. Apply both relations to real, precisely measured stars and evaluate the results against real cluster ages (previewing Lecture 09’s isochrone method).

Opening Phenomenon

Sirius A, at 2.063 M☉, is only about twice the Sun’s mass but roughly 25 times more luminous -- a wildly non-linear scaling that, this lecture shows, follows directly from how sensitively a star’s internal structure (core temperature, opacity, nuclear energy generation) responds to a change in total mass.

Vocabulary

  • mass-luminosity relation

Evidence

  • Precisely measured masses and luminosities from real eclipsing and visual binaries (ASTR 310, Lecture 09), including Alpha Centauri A and B and Sirius A/B, are well fit by a single power law L ∝ M^n with n in the range 3-4 over the main-sequence mass range relevant to this course.
  • Open star clusters (Lecture 09) with independently measured (isochrone-fit) ages show main-sequence turnoff masses that decrease systematically with increasing cluster age, exactly as the t_MS ∝ M^-2.5 lifetime scaling predicts for a coeval population of stars.
  • The most massive known main-sequence stars (tens of solar masses) are observed to have main-sequence lifetimes of only a few million years, while the lowest-mass red dwarfs are computed (from the same scaling, extrapolated) to have lifetimes far exceeding the current age of the universe -- both extremes consistent with the steep M^-2.5 scaling.

Derivation and Model

  • Homology reasoning (assuming all stars along the main sequence have structurally similar, scaled density and temperature profiles) combines hydrostatic equilibrium (P_c ∝ M²/R⁴, ASTR 310 Lecture 05), the ideal-gas law, and the radiative-diffusion luminosity (Lecture 03) to eliminate the unknown central temperature and density, yielding an approximate power-law relation L ∝ M^n with n depending on the dominant opacity source and equation-of-state regime (n ≈ 3.5 is a standard textbook approximation for electron-scattering-dominated, ideal-gas main-sequence stars).
  • A star’s total nuclear fuel supply is proportional to its mass (M), while its fuel consumption rate is proportional to its luminosity (L); the main-sequence lifetime is therefore t_MS ∝ M/L, and substituting the mass-luminosity relation gives t_MS ∝ M/M^3.5 = M^-2.5.
  • Calibrating this scaling using the Sun’s own main-sequence lifetime (≈ 10 Gyr, a standard value) as the reference point gives a simple, testable prediction for any other star’s main-sequence lifetime purely from its mass and luminosity.

Working Equation

\[ L \propto M^{3.5}, \qquad t_{\rm MS} = t_{\rm MS,\odot}\dfrac{M/M_\odot}{L/L_\odot} \propto M^{-2.5} \]

Worked Example

  1. Alpha Centauri A (1.0788 M☉): mass-luminosity prediction L ∝ M^3.5 = 1.304 L☉, compared to the independently measured 1.5059 L☉ (agreement to 13.4%); main-sequence lifetime estimate t_MS = 10 Gyr × (1.0788/1.5059) = 7.16 Gyr.
  2. Alpha Centauri B (0.9092 M☉): mass-luminosity prediction 0.717 L☉ versus measured 0.4981 L☉ (agreement to 43.9%); main-sequence lifetime estimate t_MS = 18.25 Gyr, longer than Alpha Centauri A’s despite both stars being roughly solar-mass, because B’s lower luminosity dominates the M/L ratio.
  3. Sirius A (2.063 M☉, measured L = 25.4 L☉): mass-luminosity prediction 12.6 L☉ (agreement to 50.4%, noticeably worse than for the two lower-mass Alpha Centauri stars -- expected, since the fixed n=3.5 exponent is only an approximation whose accuracy varies across the main-sequence mass range); estimated main-sequence lifetime t_MS = 0.812 Gyr, dramatically shorter than the Sun’s.

Common Misconception

Treating L ∝ M^3.5 (or any single mass-luminosity exponent) as exact across the full main-sequence mass range. The true exponent varies smoothly with mass (closer to 4 near solar mass, shallower for both very low-mass and very high-mass, radiation-pressure-supported stars), so this lecture’s single fixed exponent is a working approximation, and its accuracy should always be checked against independently measured luminosities, as done explicitly above.

Active-Learning Guidance

Using t_MS ∝ M^-2.5, predict by what factor a 10 M☉ star’s main-sequence lifetime is shorter than the Sun’s, without recomputing the full luminosity, and explain why this scaling alone (not any detail of nuclear reaction rates) is enough to conclude that massive stars are always short-lived.
