ASTR 330 · Lecture 08

Stellar Models: Polytropes and Homology Relations

A simplified but genuinely predictive family of stellar structures

Learning Goals

  1. State the polytropic equation of state P = Kρ^γ and explain why it closes the stellar-structure system with a single free index.
  2. Describe the Lane-Emden equation as the dimensionless form of hydrostatic equilibrium for a polytrope, without solving it in full.
  3. Explain how the n=3 polytrope specifically connects to both this lecture’s homology relations and the Chandrasekhar mass derived in Lecture 12.

Reading anchor: OpenStax Astronomy 2e does not develop polytropic stellar models; this course introduces the Lane-Emden formalism specifically to prepare for the Chandrasekhar-mass derivation in Lecture 12.

Why This Matters

Lecture 07’s mass-luminosity relation used homology reasoning informally; polytropic stellar models make that reasoning mathematically precise by replacing the full, coupled equation of state and opacity law with a single simplified pressure-density relation, at the cost of realism. The n=3 polytrope in particular reappears explicitly and quantitatively in Lecture 12’s Chandrasekhar-mass derivation.

Opening Phenomenon

A fully convective star (Lecture 04) has a nearly adiabatic, and therefore nearly polytropic, structure throughout its interior, while a relativistically degenerate white dwarf (Lecture 12) is described by an equation of state that is exactly polytropic with index n=3 -- two very different physical stars, both well described by the same simplified mathematical framework developed in this lecture.

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Polytropic models with n=3 (the "Eddington standard model") were historically used, before the advent of numerical stellar-evolution codes, to make quantitatively successful predictions for the internal density and pressure structure of stars like the Sun, despite the model’s simplified equation of state.
  • The n=3/2 polytrope (P ∝ ρ^(5/3)) is found, both historically and in modern white-dwarf models, to accurately describe the interior of a fully non-relativistically-degenerate white dwarf, a direct precursor to Lecture 12’s Chandrasekhar-mass derivation.
  • The relativistically degenerate n=3 polytrope (P ∝ ρ^(4/3)) is the specific equation of state whose associated Lane-Emden solution produces a mass that is completely independent of the star’s central density -- exactly the mathematical origin of the Chandrasekhar mass’s remarkable universality, derived quantitatively in Lecture 12.

Derivation and Model

  • A polytrope assumes a simplified equation of state P = Kρ^γ = Kρ^{(n+1)/n}, with K a constant and n the polytropic index; substituting this into hydrostatic equilibrium and mass continuity produces a single second-order differential equation (the Lane-Emden equation) in a dimensionless radius and a dimensionless density function θ, with n as the only free parameter.
  • Different physical regimes correspond to different polytropic indices: n=3/2 for a non-relativistic, fully degenerate electron gas or for a fully convective (adiabatic, monatomic ideal gas) stellar envelope; n=3 for a relativistically degenerate electron gas or for a radiation-pressure-dominated, very massive star (the Eddington standard model).
  • For a fixed polytropic index n, the Lane-Emden equation’s solution fixes dimensionless relations between the star’s total mass, radius, and central density that depend only on n (not on the star’s particular mass) -- these are the homology relations, and for n=3 specifically, the resulting mass-radius relation is independent of central density altogether, the key mathematical fact exploited in Lecture 12.

Quantitative Tool

\[ P = K\rho^{(n+1)/n}, \qquad \dfrac{1}{\xi^{2}}\dfrac{d}{d\xi}\left(\xi^{2}\dfrac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right) = -\theta^{n}\ \ (\text{Lane-Emden equation, dimensionless}) \]

Worked Example

  1. Sun-like fully convective envelope approximation: n=3/2 polytrope (γ = 5/3, matching the ideal monatomic adiabatic gradient ∇_ad = 2/5 derived in Lecture 04), consistent with the Sun’s own convective outer envelope (r > 0.71 R☉) being reasonably close to adiabatic.
  2. Eddington standard model: n=3 polytrope (γ = 4/3), historically applied to the Sun and other main-sequence stars as a first approximation combining radiation pressure with a simplified opacity law -- a precursor to this course’s more complete four-equation treatment (Lecture 01) with a realistic, non-polytropic equation of state and opacity law.
  3. The n=3 polytrope’s central-density-independent mass, M_n=3 = 4π(K/πG)^(3/2)(-ξ² dθ/dξ)|_{ξ_1}, is precisely the mathematical structure Lecture 12 uses (with K set by relativistic electron degeneracy pressure rather than an arbitrary constant) to derive the Chandrasekhar mass of 1.435 M☉.

Common Misconception

Assuming a polytropic model is a realistic stellar-structure solution for an arbitrary star. A polytrope’s single fixed exponent γ cannot simultaneously capture a star’s actual, depth-varying equation of state, opacity, and energy generation; polytropic models are most reliable either as historical/pedagogical approximations (the Eddington standard model) or in genuinely polytropic physical regimes (fully convective envelopes, fully degenerate matter), not as general-purpose precision stellar models.

Active Learning Segment

Given that the n=3 polytrope’s mass is independent of central density, while lower-n polytropes’ masses are not, explain in words why this single mathematical fact is exactly what makes the Chandrasekhar mass (Lecture 12) a fixed number rather than a family of possible white-dwarf masses.

Synthesis

Polytropic models trade full physical realism for mathematical tractability by fixing a single equation-of-state exponent, and while this makes them inadequate as general precision stellar models, the specific n=3 case supplies the exact mathematical structure this course needs in Lecture 12 to derive the Chandrasekhar mass.
