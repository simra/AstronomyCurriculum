Lecture 09: Post-Main-Sequence Evolution: Shell Burning and the Red Giant Branch

ASTR 330 Astrophysics II: Stellar Structure and Evolution

Context and Why This Matters

Every star this course has discussed so far (Alpha Centauri A/B, Sirius A, the Sun) is still on the main sequence; this lecture is the pivot point where the course turns from "how a star maintains equilibrium" to "how a star’s equilibrium structure changes as its core composition changes," beginning the evolutionary story that occupies the rest of the course.

Learning Goals

  1. Explain why core hydrogen exhaustion leads to core contraction and envelope expansion rather than simply the star turning off.
  2. Describe hydrogen shell burning and the physical origin of the Schönberg-Chandrasekhar limit for an isothermal helium core.
  3. Apply the main-sequence lifetime scaling (Lecture 07) and a real star cluster’s main-sequence turnoff to estimate a cluster’s age, and honestly assess the result against the cluster’s independently measured age.

Opening Phenomenon

The Hyades open cluster, whose distance (47 pc) and age (625 Myr) were live-verified this session, shows a well-defined main-sequence turnoff at a stellar mass of about 2.3 M☉: stars above this mass have already evolved into subgiants, giants, or white dwarfs, while less massive stars remain on the main sequence -- a direct, real-world illustration of the mass-dependent stellar lifetime derived in Lecture 07.

Vocabulary

  • main-sequence turnoff

  • red giant branch
  • Schönberg-Chandrasekhar limit
  • core-envelope decoupling

Evidence

  • Every star cluster’s stars are, to good approximation, coeval (formed at essentially the same time from the same molecular cloud), so a cluster’s color-magnitude diagram directly shows which masses have already evolved off the main sequence, providing the isochrone method for dating clusters used throughout this lecture and Lab 05.
  • Detailed stellar-evolution models of a hydrogen-exhausted stellar core reproduce the observed rapid radius expansion and luminosity increase that defines the red giant branch, driven by a thin, extremely temperature-sensitive hydrogen-burning shell surrounding a contracting, inert helium core.
  • Stars found in the Schönberg-Chandrasekhar-limit-violating regime (isothermal helium core mass exceeding roughly 10% of the star’s total mass) are observed to undergo rapid core contraction and envelope expansion on a short (thermal, not nuclear) timescale, consistent with the loss of hydrostatic support once the limit is exceeded.

Derivation and Model

  • Once core hydrogen is exhausted, the core (now composed of inert helium, with no local energy generation) begins to contract under its own gravity, while hydrogen fusion continues in a thin shell surrounding the core, where fresh hydrogen is still present at a temperature high enough for pp-chain or CNO-cycle burning (Lectures 05-06).
  • The core, receiving essentially no energy generation of its own, cannot use radiative energy transport to remain in a stable temperature profile; instead it approaches a roughly isothermal state, and Schönberg and Chandrasekhar showed that an isothermal core in hydrostatic equilibrium can only support a limited fraction (≈ 10%, depending on composition) of the star’s total mass before hydrostatic equilibrium fails and the core must contract rapidly.
  • As the core contracts and heats (releasing gravitational potential energy, per the virial theorem of ASTR 310 Lecture 06), the hydrogen-burning shell above it also heats and expands, driving a dramatic increase in the star’s radius and luminosity that defines its ascent up the red giant branch.

\[ \left(\dfrac{M_{\rm core}}{M_\star}\right)_{\rm SC} \approx 0.37\left(\dfrac{\mu_{\rm env}}{\mu_{\rm core}}\right)^{2}\ \ (\text{Sch\"onberg--Chandrasekhar limit, schematic}) \]

Worked Example

  1. Hyades main-sequence turnoff mass (live-verified this session): 2.3 M☉. Mass-luminosity prediction (Lecture 07): L ≈ M^3.5 = 18.45 L☉.
  2. Predicted main-sequence lifetime for a 2.3 M☉ star: t_MS = 10 Gyr × (M/L) = 1.246 Gyr = 1246 Myr.
  3. Actual, independently measured (isochrone-fit) Hyades cluster age (live-verified this session, Perryman et al. 1998): 625 Myr = 0.625 Gyr. The predicted turnoff lifetime is about 2.0 times larger than the cluster’s actual age -- a genuine, factor-of-two-scale discrepancy, not close agreement. This is an expected consequence of the fixed n=3.5 mass-luminosity exponent and the Sun-calibrated lifetime constant both being approximations (Lecture 07’s pitfall): a more accurate mass-dependent exponent and a lifetime constant calibrated near 2-3 M☉ rather than at 1 M☉ would bring the two closer together, but this lecture reports the actual size of the discrepancy honestly rather than describing it as good agreement.

Common Misconception

Describing this lecture’s factor-of-two turnoff-age discrepancy as "excellent agreement" or glossing over its size. A rough order-of-magnitude scaling relation reproducing a real cluster’s age to within a factor of about two is a genuine success for such a simple model, but it is not close quantitative agreement, and claiming otherwise would repeat exactly the kind of overstatement the ASTR 310 review process explicitly corrected.

Active-Learning Guidance

Given that the Hyades turnoff-mass lifetime prediction overshoots the cluster’s actual age by about a factor of two, and knowing the true mass-luminosity exponent is not a fixed 3.5 across all masses, explain qualitatively whether a steeper true exponent near 2-3 M☉ would bring the predicted lifetime closer to or further from the measured age.
