ASTR 120 Problem Set 05 Solution Key

Instructor-facing worked solutions

Instructor Notes

Reward careful comparison rather than memorized planet facts. Ocean evidence is indirect; students should identify ambiguity in each measurement.

Problem 1. Giant-Planet Density and Composition

Worked solution: Jupiter: \(318/11.2^3=0.226\rho_E=1.25\,\mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}\). Saturn: \(95/9.45^3=0.113\rho_E=0.62\,\mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}\), less dense than water. Uranus: \(14.5/4.01^3=0.225\rho_E=1.24\,\mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}\). Neptune: \(17.1/3.88^3=0.293\rho_E=1.61\,\mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}\). Low density means large volume relative to mass, not small mass. Uranus and Neptune are denser than Saturn and comparable/higher than Jupiter despite much lower mass, consistent with larger fractions of ice/rock and smaller H/He envelopes.

Problem 2. Sunlight and Internal Heat

Worked solution: Relative sunlight is \(1/r^2\). Jupiter: \(1/5.2^2=0.037\). Saturn: \(1/9.5^2=0.011\). Uranus: \(1/19.2^2=0.0027\). Neptune: \(1/30^2=0.0011\). Neptune emitting 2.6 times absorbed sunlight implies a substantial internal heat source; Uranus emitting roughly absorbed sunlight implies much weaker internal heat escape or different interior structure. Thermal infrared measurements, energy-balance comparisons, cloud-top temperatures, and atmospheric dynamics help distinguish absorbed solar energy from internal heat.

Problem 3. Europa Ocean Evidence Chain

Worked solution: Induced magnetic signal is most direct: a conducting salty layer can respond to Jupiter's changing magnetic environment; ambiguity includes plasma interaction or ionosphere assumptions. Young fractured surface suggests resurfacing and mobile ice over a warmer interior; ambiguity includes impact gardening or solid-state convection without a global ocean. Plumes would sample subsurface material if confirmed; ambiguity includes detection limits, viewing geometry, or surface sputtering. Tidal heating from eccentricity maintained by resonance supplies energy that can keep subsurface water liquid despite weak sunlight.

Problem 4. Ring-Particle Orbital Periods

Worked solution: \(P_{outer}/P_{inner}=(137000/75000)^{3/2}=1.827^{1.5}=2.47\). If the inner period is 5.0 h, the outer period is \(12.3\) h. Different orbital periods create shear because inner particles lap outer particles. Collisions, spreading, and resonances can alter ring structure; narrow rings therefore need confinement by shepherd moons, resonant forcing, or replenishment from source material.

Common errors: using linear radius ratio as period ratio, or treating rings as rigid disks.
