Instructor-facing worked solutions

Instructor Notes

Reward caution and traceability. Students may choose different mission architectures if the measurement chain is coherent and planetary-protection implications are explicit.

Problem 1. Habitability Evidence Matrix

Worked solution: Strong answers usually score liquid environment 1-2 because an induced magnetic signal suggests a conductive layer but does not uniquely prove an ocean. Energy source can score 1-2 because resonance supports tidal heating, but magnitude is unknown. Chemistry can score 1 because salts near fractures suggest exchange or brines but not full nutrient availability. Stability may score 0-1 because surface temperature is very low and radiation is harsh, though subsurface shielding may help. Evidence quality may score 1 because plume detection is single-event and needs confirmation. Conclusion should say the moon is a plausible habitability target, not a life detection.

Common errors: treating habitability as confirmed life or ignoring radiation and evidence quality.

Problem 2. Insolation and Habitable-Zone Caution

Worked solution: Planet 1: \(S/S_E=0.25/(0.50)^2=1.0\). Planet 2: \(S/S_E=1.0/(1.60)^2=0.391\). The first receives Earth-like flux; the second receives about 39% of Earth's flux. Flux alone is insufficient because atmosphere, albedo, greenhouse effect, rotation, volatile inventory, stellar activity, planetary mass/radius, ocean/land distribution, and geologic cycling matter. Follow-up measurements include radius, mass/density, atmosphere detection/composition, albedo/temperature, stellar activity spectrum, orbit/eccentricity, water indicators, and surface pressure constraints.

Earth comparison41.0 and 0.39.
Flux limitation6Atmosphere/greenhouse/etc.
Follow-up measurements7Four specific measurements.

Common errors: calling any Earth-flux planet habitable or omitting atmospheric pressure/composition.

Problem 3. Planetary Protection Risk Chain

Worked solution: After cleaning, \(3.0\times10^4/10^3=30\) viable spores remain. After cruise radiation, \(30/50=0.60\) viable organisms are expected at landing. With a 10% chance of reaching a warm, wet niche, expected niche delivery is \(0.60\times0.10=0.060\). Even less than one expected organism matters because forward contamination could compromise life-detection experiments, confuse biosignatures, and alter protected environments. Risk-reducing choices include stricter sterilization, cleanroom assembly, sealed sample paths, impact-avoidance trajectories, terminal disposal away from special regions, and redundant containment.

CriterionPointsNotes

Niche-delivery estimate5Expected value 0.060.

Mission design mitigations7Two specific choices.

Common errors: rounding 0.60 to zero without interpretation or treating planetary protection as only ethical, not scientific.

Problem 4. Mission Traceability Table

Worked solution: A strong concept might choose repeated plume flybys. Investigation 1: mass spectrometer, measure water vapor, salts, organics, and isotopes; uncertainty is contamination and plume variability. Investigation 2: dust analyzer, measure ice grain composition; uncertainty is grain alteration during collection. Investigation 3: imaging/thermal mapper, locate active vents and heat anomalies; uncertainty is viewing geometry and temporal variability. A flyby is lower risk and can sample plume material without landing, but gives limited temporal coverage; an orbiter improves coverage at higher cost and radiation/orbit complexity. A disconfirming measurement would be plume composition matching surface sputtering without ocean salts, or no repeat plume activity. Planetary protection requires clean sampling hardware and impact/disposal planning.

CriterionPointsNotes

Architecture tradeoff5Justified choice.
Disconfirming validation5Must be a real contrary result.
Planetary protection5Architecture-specific.

Common errors: listing instruments without measurements, or designing a mission that cannot answer the science question.
