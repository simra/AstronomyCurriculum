Terrestrial Planet Surfaces

Solar-system evidence, physics, and exploration

Why This Question Matters

The Moon became a calibration surface after Apollo; Mars orbiters and rovers made comparative geology quantitative.

Learning Goals

  • Use surface features to infer relative age and geological process.
  • Connect a mission or observation to the physical interpretation.
  • Use a quantitative or comparative argument with explicit assumptions.

Terms for This Lecture

Impact crater

Definition: A roughly circular depression excavated when a projectile transfers energy and momentum to a planetary surface.

Why it matters and connects: Crater size, morphology, ejecta, and abundance record impact conditions, target properties, and surface history.

Boundary or common confusion: Crater diameter is not the projectile diameter, and later erosion or burial can erase the record.

Crater density

Definition: The number of craters above a stated size per unit surface area.

Why it matters and connects: When impact flux and preservation are comparable, higher crater density generally indicates longer exposure and an older surface.

Boundary or common confusion: It is a relative clock unless calibrated, and resurfacing, saturation, and image resolution can bias counts.

Resurfacing

Definition: Any process that buries, erodes, deforms, or replaces an older surface and resets part of its visible crater record.

Why it matters and connects: Volcanism, tectonics, sedimentation, glacial flow, and impacts can create young-looking terrain on an old world.

Boundary or common confusion: Few craters date the exposed surface, not necessarily the planet or underlying rock.

Stratigraphy

Definition: The ordering of geologic units and events using superposition, cross-cutting, embayment, and other spatial relationships.

Why it matters and connects: It reconstructs relative history even when absolute ages or samples are unavailable.

Boundary or common confusion: Local stratigraphic order may not transfer globally without correlation evidence.

Volcanism

Definition: The transport and eruption of molten or mobilized interior material onto or near a surface.

Why it matters and connects: Volcanic plains, shields, domes, and gases reveal internal heat, composition, and pathways through the lithosphere.

Boundary or common confusion: Smooth terrain is not automatically volcanic; morphology, spectra, and context must rule out sedimentary or impact deposits.

Tectonics

Definition: Deformation of a planetary lithosphere by extension, compression, shear, loading, contraction, or convection.

Why it matters and connects: Faults, folds, rifts, ridges, and scarps reveal stress and interior evolution.

Boundary or common confusion: Tectonics does not imply Earth-like plate tectonics; most worlds deform without mobile global plates.

Opening Phenomenon

Craters, volcanoes, tectonics, and erosion make planetary surfaces historical records.

Surfaces Preserve Competing Processes

Impacts: add craters and expose subsurface material.

Volcanism and tectonics: erase, deform, or bury older terrain.

Weathering: changes slopes, brightness, and small-scale texture over time.

A Surface Is a Geological Argument

When you inspect a planetary surface, ask which process made the feature, which process modified it, and whether the observed terrain is old enough to preserve the evidence.

Evidence We Need

  • Cratered highlands preserve older surfaces.
  • Smooth plains can indicate lava flooding or resurfacing.
  • Tectonic features record stress and interior evolution.

Crater Density as a Relative Clock

Compare the two terrains only after checking the mapped area and resolution. A younger volcanic plain can have fewer craters because lava buried the older record.

Quantitative Surface Test

\[ N={\mathrm{craters}\over \mathrm{mapped\ area}} \]

If one 10,000 km2 region has 80 counted craters and another has 8 at the same detection threshold, the first has ten times the crater density. That supports an older exposure age unless resurfacing or preservation differs.

Worked Interpretation: Few Craters, Many Meanings

  • Few craters on a lava plain can mean recent volcanism.
  • Few craters on a steep slope can mean erosion or mass wasting.
  • Few craters in a low-resolution image can simply mean the craters are below detection.

Evidence Comparison: Shape Versus Context

A channel-shaped feature suggests flow, but context determines the fluid or process: lava channels, water-carved valleys, collapse troughs, and tectonic rifts can all produce elongated forms.

Surface Pitfall: Counting Without Calibration

Crater counts give relative ages most cleanly when impact flux, target properties, erosion, resurfacing, and detection threshold are comparable. Absolute ages require calibration, not just a crater tally.

Reasoning Prompt: Old Highlands or Young Plains?

You are given two equal-area image tiles: one densely cratered and rough, one smooth with lobate margins and wrinkle ridges. Build a two-step argument for which is older and name one observation that could overturn your interpretation.

Synthesis: Surfaces Are Histories

  • Features record formation, modification, and preservation.
  • Crater density is powerful only with scale and resurfacing context.
  • Robust geological interpretations compare landform shape, cross-cutting relations, and physical process.
