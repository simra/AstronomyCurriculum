ASTR 120 · Lecture 07

The Moon, Mercury, and Airless Worlds

Solar-system evidence, physics, and exploration

Why This Question Matters

Lunar samples and crater chronology anchor much of inner solar-system history.

Learning Goals

  • Interpret airless surfaces using craters, rays, regolith, and composition.
  • Connect a mission or observation to the physical interpretation.
  • Use a quantitative or comparative argument with explicit assumptions.

Opening Phenomenon

Airless bodies preserve impact histories and space-weathering records with minimal atmospheric erosion.

Airless Worlds Keep Surface Records

Regolith: broken, gardened surface material produced by repeated impacts.

Crater rays: bright ejecta that darkens and matures with exposure.

Space weathering: micrometeorites and solar wind alter color and reflectance.

Why the Moon and Mercury Are Not Simple Twins

Both are airless and cratered, but Mercury's higher solar heating, larger metal fraction, and stronger solar environment affect volatile retention, surface chemistry, and tectonic history.

Evidence We Need

  • Airless surfaces retain craters and space-weathered regolith.
  • Mercury has high density and a large metal fraction.
  • Lunar samples anchor crater chronology.

Crater Rays as Relative Youth Markers

Bright rays mark material excavated and spread across older terrain. The interpretation depends on impact freshness, composition contrast, and how quickly space weathering darkens exposed grains.

Quantitative Clock: Crater Density with Caution

\[ \mathrm{relative\ exposure\ age}\uparrow\quad \mathrm{as}\quad {N_{craters}\over A}\uparrow \]

For two lunar plains imaged at the same resolution, a higher crater density usually means longer exposure. The clock fails if one surface was buried, saturated, or counted with a different detection threshold.

Worked Case: Fresh Crater or Compositional Patch?

  • Bright rays around a crisp crater support recent excavation.
  • A bright area without radial geometry could be different composition or illumination.
  • Color ratios and topography help distinguish freshness from material differences.

Evidence Boundary: Volatiles in Cold Traps

Airless does not mean volatile-free. Permanently shadowed polar regions can preserve ice if temperatures remain low and delivery exceeds loss.

Reasoning Prompt: Moon Versus Mercury

You observe a permanently shadowed polar crater and a radar-bright deposit. What evidence would distinguish clean water ice, ice mixed with regolith, and a rough rocky surface?

Evidence Bridge: Regolith Records Exposure

Airless regolith changes with micrometeorite gardening and solar-wind implantation. Maturity indicators help separate a genuinely young crater from an older surface that only looks bright because composition or illumination differs.

Synthesis: Airless Surfaces Are Exposure Experiments

  • Impact features supply relative ages and excavation clues.
  • Space weathering changes optical properties over time.
  • Composition and temperature determine whether volatiles can survive without an atmosphere.
