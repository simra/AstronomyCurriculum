Connection to the Course

Lecture 04 showed that interiors provide energy and structure. Surfaces are where interior processes, impacts, atmosphere, and time become visible. Lecture 06 will extend this reasoning to atmospheres and climate, where erosion and weathering can erase or modify surface records.

Surfaces as Historical Records

Terrestrial planet surfaces preserve sequences of events. A cratered highland, a lava plain, a fault scarp, and a river channel each point to a different process. Relative dating uses logical order: a feature that cuts another feature is younger; a smooth lava flow that covers craters is younger than the craters it buries; a crater on top of a volcanic plain is younger than the plain.

Crater counting is powerful because impactors accumulate over time, but it works only when preservation is comparable. A heavily cratered region is usually older than a lightly cratered region at the same image resolution and under similar geologic conditions. Atmospheres, volcanism, tectonics, sedimentation, and erosion can reset or obscure the crater clock.

Terminology Support

  • Crater density: number of craters per unit area above a specified diameter; a relative age indicator when calibrated carefully.
  • Resurfacing: any process that covers, erases, or modifies older terrain, including lava flooding, erosion, sedimentation, and tectonics.
  • Tectonics: deformation of the crust by stress, expressed as faults, folds, rifts, scarps, or ridges.
  • Stratigraphic relationship: the order implied by overlapping, cutting, embaying, or burial of features.

Worked Quantitative Example: Relative Crater Density

Suppose two regions are imaged at the same resolution. Region A has 80 craters larger than \(1\,\text{km}\) in an area of \(10{,}000\,\text{km}^2\); region B has 12 such craters in the same area. The crater densities are

\[N_A=\frac{80}{10{,}000}=8.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{km}^{-2},\qquad N_B=1.2\times10^{-3}\,\text{km}^{-2}.\]

Region A has about \(N_A/N_B\approx6.7\) times the crater density of region B, so it is likely older or better preserved. The conclusion assumes comparable target properties, impactor flux, detection threshold, and resurfacing history.

Observational Evidence

The Moon provides a calibration surface because Apollo samples tie radiometric ages to mapped terrains. Mars orbiters add high-resolution imaging, topography, mineral maps, and rover ground truth. Mercury, Venus, and the Moon show how surface records differ when atmospheres, temperatures, and interiors differ. Visual interpretation should always include scale bars, illumination direction, image resolution, and the possibility that similar-looking features formed by different processes.

Common Misconceptions and Traps

  • Few craters does not always mean young. Erosion or burial can remove craters.
  • Smooth does not mean inactive. A smooth plain may be volcanic, sedimentary, icy, or impact-melt related.
  • Crater counting is not absolute without calibration. Relative ages need sample ages or flux models for absolute dates.
  • One image is not enough. Topography, spectra, and context often change the interpretation.

Study-Ready Synthesis

Surface geology turns planetary images into histories. The key is to identify the process, order events, and state what could erase or mimic the observed feature. Crater counts provide a clock, but only with preservation and calibration carefully stated.
