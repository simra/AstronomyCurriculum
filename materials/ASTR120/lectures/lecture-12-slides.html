Exoplanetary Systems in Solar-System Context

Solar-system evidence, physics, and exploration

Why This Question Matters

Radial-velocity and transit surveys revealed hot Jupiters, super-Earths, and compact systems that forced revisions to formation theory.

Learning Goals

  • Compare exoplanet architectures with solar-system formation expectations.
  • Connect a mission or observation to the physical interpretation.

Terms for This Lecture

Transit

Definition: A temporary decrease in observed stellar brightness when a planet crosses the star's visible disk.

Why it matters and connects: Transit timing, duration, and depth constrain orbital period, geometry, and planet-to-star radius ratio.

Boundary or common confusion: A brightness dip can have stellar or instrumental causes, and most planetary systems are not aligned to transit.

\(\delta\approx(R_p/R_\star)^2\)

Radial velocity

Definition: The line-of-sight component of a star's motion inferred from Doppler shifts in its spectrum.

Why it matters and connects: A periodic stellar velocity signal measures the gravitational reflex motion caused by an orbiting companion.

Boundary or common confusion: It yields a minimum mass unless orbital inclination is known and can be confused by stellar activity.

\(\Delta\lambda/\lambda\approx v_r/c\)

Detection bias

Definition: A systematic preference for discovering planets whose signals are larger, more frequent, or easier to separate from noise.

Why it matters and connects: It must be modeled before using detected planets to infer the true population or compare architectures with the solar system.

Boundary or common confusion: A catalog distribution is not automatically the underlying occurrence distribution.

Minimum mass

Definition: The lower bound \(M_p\sin i\) obtained from radial velocity when orbital inclination \(i\) is unknown.

Why it matters and connects: It states precisely what the Doppler data constrain before transit, astrometry, or dynamics provide geometry.

Boundary or common confusion: The true mass can be larger, especially for nearly face-on systems.

\(M_{\rm min}=M_p\sin i\)

Planet occurrence rate

Definition: The average number or fraction of planets in a defined radius, mass, period, and host-star range after correcting for survey completeness.

Why it matters and connects: Occurrence rates allow a fairer comparison between the solar system and exoplanet populations than raw discovery counts.

Boundary or common confusion: The value depends on bin definitions, stellar sample, detection efficiency, and assumptions about missed planets.

Planetary migration

Definition: A change in orbital size caused by exchange of angular momentum with a gas disk, planetesimal disk, or other planets.

Why it matters and connects: Migration explains why hot Jupiters and compact systems can contain planets that likely formed elsewhere.

Boundary or common confusion: Migration is a family of mechanisms, not evidence that every planet moved by the same amount or direction.

Opening Phenomenon

Exoplanets test whether our solar system is typical or unusual.

Exoplanets Test Solar-System Assumptions

Transits: planet size from fractional starlight blocked.

Radial velocity: planet mass constraints from stellar wobble.

Demographics: populations reveal architectures the solar system does not contain.

The Solar System Is One Outcome

Hot Jupiters, compact multi-planet systems, and super-Earths show that planet formation and migration produce a wider range of architectures than our local example alone suggested.

Evidence We Need

  • Hot Jupiters exist very close to their stars.
  • Transit and radial velocity methods favor different planets.
  • Compact multi-planet systems challenge simple solar-system analogies.

Transit Depth Calculation

\[ \delta\approx\left({R_p\over R_*}\right)^2 \]
  • A Jupiter-radius planet transiting a Sun-like star blocks about 1 percent of the light.
  • An Earth-radius planet transiting a Sun-like star blocks about 0.008 percent.
  • The same Earth-size planet is easier to detect around a smaller star.

Worked Architecture Comparison

A close-in giant planet is hard to reconcile with simple in-place frost-line formation. It points toward migration, scattering, or disk-driven movement after formation.

Evidence Pairing: Radius Needs Mass

Transit data can give size, but density requires mass. A 2-Earth-radius planet could be rocky, water-rich, or gas-enveloped unless radial velocity or transit timing helps constrain mass.

Exoplanet Pitfall: Discovery Counts Are Not Occurrence Rates

A catalog dominated by easy detections does not mean the universe is dominated by those planets. Survey geometry, cadence, stellar type, and noise all filter the sample.

Reasoning Prompt: Solar-System Context

You discover a compact system of five sub-Neptunes inside Mercury's orbit. Which solar-system formation expectation does it challenge most: frost-line composition, long-term stability, or migration history?

Synthesis: One System Becomes a Comparative Baseline

  • The solar system supplies physical intuition and constraints.
  • Exoplanets reveal outcomes absent from our local architecture.
  • Detection methods must be modeled before population claims are made.
