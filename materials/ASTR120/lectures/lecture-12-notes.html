Lecture 12: Exoplanetary Systems in Solar-System Context

ASTR 120 Solar System

Learning Objectives

  1. Compare exoplanet architectures with expectations from solar-system formation models.
  2. Explain how transit and radial-velocity methods bias the planet sample we discover first.
  3. Use transit depth and period information to infer basic planet properties and detection limits.

Connection to the Course

The previous lectures developed a solar-system reference model. Exoplanets test which parts of that model are universal and which are local outcomes. Hot Jupiters, compact multi-planet systems, and super-Earths show that the solar system is one case in a broader population.

Detection Before Demographics

Exoplanet interpretation begins with detection method. Transits measure the slight dimming when a planet crosses its star, favoring large planets close to stars and systems aligned edge-on. Radial velocity measures stellar motion along the line of sight, favoring massive planets close to their stars. These methods revealed planets unlike any in the solar system, including hot Jupiters and compact systems of close-in planets.

Because detection is biased, demographics must be corrected before making claims about what planets are common. The absence of detected solar-system analogs in an early survey may reflect method sensitivity rather than actual rarity.

Terminology Support

  • Transit: temporary reduction in starlight when a planet crosses in front of its star.
  • Radial velocity: line-of-sight stellar motion inferred from Doppler shifts caused by orbiting planets.
  • Hot Jupiter: Jupiter-size planet orbiting very close to its star.
  • Demographics: statistical properties of planet populations after accounting for detection biases.

Worked Quantitative Example: Transit Depth

Transit depth is approximately \((R_p/R_*)^2\). For a Jupiter-size planet with \(R_p\approx0.10R_{\odot}\) crossing a Sun-like star,

\[\delta\approx(0.10)^2=0.010=1.0\%.\]

For an Earth-size planet with \(R_p\approx0.0092R_{\odot}\),

\[\delta\approx(0.0092)^2\approx8.5\times10^{-5}\approx0.0085\%.\]

This explains why large, close-in planets were easier to find early: their signals are deeper and repeat more often.

Observational Evidence

Transit surveys provide radii and orbital periods for many planets. Radial-velocity surveys provide mass constraints and orbital information. When both methods apply, density can be estimated, allowing comparison with rocky, icy, and gas-rich worlds. Exoplanet systems challenge simple solar-system analogies because many have close-in super-Earths or mini-Neptunes absent from our solar system.

Common Misconceptions and Traps

  • Detected does not mean common. Detection efficiency shapes the observed sample.
  • No transit does not mean no planet. The orbit may not be aligned with our line of sight.
  • Radius alone is not composition. Mass and atmosphere matter for density and interpretation.
  • The solar system is not the template for every system. It is a detailed comparison case.

Study-Ready Synthesis

Exoplanets extend comparative planetology beyond one system. The central discipline is to correct for detection bias before interpreting architecture. Hot Jupiters and compact systems show that migration and disk diversity are important, while solar-system evidence remains the high-detail anchor for physical processes.
