ASTR 472 Lecture 08: Calibration II: Error Budgets and Imaging Systematics

Radio Astronomy · study notes · 400-level elective

Learning objectives

  1. Propagate independent fractional calibration terms in quadrature.
  2. Separate thermal noise from multiplicative and spatially correlated systematics.
  3. Design residual and jackknife checks for a calibrated image.

Prerequisites in use: ASTR 340; PHYS 341 electromagnetism is recommended for receiver and radiation physics.

Conceptual entry point

A formal image rms is not a complete uncertainty when the flux scale, primary beam, and deconvolution add correlated errors. Begin with the receiver product: a spectrum, time series, image, or set of complex visibilities. The object we ultimately discuss is several inference steps removed from that product.

Radio astronomy's history makes this distinction concrete. Karl Jansky's 1930s rotating antenna detected persistent celestial interference while he investigated radio communication noise; Grote Reber then built a purpose-designed reflector and mapped the radio sky. Instrumental curiosity became a new window on the Galaxy.

Evidence and measurement layers

  • Independent small fractional errors combine in quadrature, whereas shared calibration errors correlate many image pixels.
  • Primary-beam correction amplifies both signal and noise toward the field edge.
  • Residual images, calibrator closure quantities, split-data comparisons, and injection tests probe different failure modes.

A calibrated brightness scale is not equivalent to a flux measurement, and a catalog parameter is not equivalent to a direct observable. State the processing stage whenever comparing results.

Derivation and assumptions

For a derived flux S=f(x_i), first-order propagation gives sigma_S^2=sum_i (partial f/partial x_i)^2 sigma_i^2 plus covariance terms. For independent fractional terms f_j, (sigma_S/S)^2=sum_j f_j^2. A multiplicative flux-scale term remains correlated among sources calibrated with the same solution and should not be averaged down as independent pixel noise.

Observational interpretation

Primary-beam correction amplifies both signal and noise toward the field edge. A defensible interpretation records the competing hypotheses, checks whether the instrument response can mimic the feature, and identifies a measurement that would separate the alternatives. The next layer of evidence should be chosen for discriminating power, not simply because it is available.

Common conceptual traps

  • Confusing total flux with flux per beam, or a brightness temperature with a thermodynamic temperature.
  • Assuming a narrow thermal-noise estimate includes flux-scale or selection uncertainty.
  • Treating spatial resolution, field of view, and largest recoverable scale as interchangeable.
  • Using a Doppler or calibration convention without recording its reference frame.

Study and transfer questions

  1. Two sources share the same 5% flux-scale error but have independent 1% thermal errors. Which uncertainty cancels in their flux ratio, and which does not?
  2. Change the dominant input by 10%. Predict the direction of the output change before computing it.
  3. Name one raw diagnostic and one independent data product that could falsify the preferred interpretation.
