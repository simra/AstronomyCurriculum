Lecture 01: The Radio Sky: Emission Mechanisms and Observables

Radio Astronomy

Radio maps show jets, cold gas, and coherent pulses that can be faint or invisible in optical images.

Objectives and route

  1. Distinguish thermal free-free, synchrotron, coherent, and line emission by spectral and polarization signatures.
  2. Relate synchrotron characteristic frequency to electron energy and magnetic field.
  3. Separate measured flux density from source brightness and physical interpretation.

Route: identify what the receiver measures, derive the physical/instrument relation, calculate a scale, then test its assumptions against a diagnostic.

Observation before interpretation

Radio maps show jets, cold gas, and coherent pulses that can be faint or invisible in optical images.

  • A power-law continuum is consistent with optically thin synchrotron emission, but spectral curvature can signal cooling or absorption.
  • Thermal bremsstrahlung can be optically thin or turn over at low frequency; a flat spectrum alone does not uniquely identify a mechanism.
  • Radio data preserve frequency, polarization, and time structure that an integrated broadband flux would discard.

Keep four layers distinct in your notebook: raw correlation or spectrum, calibrated product, model parameter, and physical claim.

Physical model

For a relativistic electron with Lorentz factor gamma in magnetic field B, the characteristic synchrotron frequency scales as nu_c = (3/2) gamma^2 (e B sin(alpha))/(2 pi m_e). Averaging pitch angle gives the teaching approximation nu_c approximately 4.2e6 B_G gamma^2 Hz. It is a characteristic frequency, not a monochromatic emission line.

\(\nu_c\simeq 4.2\times10^6 B_{\rm G}\gamma^2\ {\rm Hz}\)

Define each quantity and convention before substitution. A relation is useful only inside its stated regime.

Worked calculation

Inputs: B=1e-05 G, gamma=10000.

nu_c = 4.2e+09 Hz

The single-electron characteristic frequency ignores the broad synchrotron kernel, pitch-angle distribution, and self-absorption; a measured spectral peak is not automatically nu_c.

Where the inference can fail

The single-electron characteristic frequency ignores the broad synchrotron kernel, pitch-angle distribution, and self-absorption; a measured spectral peak is not automatically nu_c.

  • Check the frequency, velocity frame, beam definition, and calibration convention before comparing values.
  • Use residuals and alternate models to distinguish a weak signal from a plausible but non-unique interpretation.
  • Report thermal noise separately from calibration, selection, and model uncertainty.
