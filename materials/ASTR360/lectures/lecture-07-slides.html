The Molecular Interstellar Medium: H2 Formation and CO as a Tracer

Why astronomers map an invisible molecule using a rare, visible one

Learning Goals

  1. Explain why molecular hydrogen (H2), despite being the most abundant molecule in molecular clouds, cannot be directly observed at the low temperatures typical of star-forming clouds, and why grain-surface formation dominates over gas-phase formation.
  2. Derive the CO-to-H2 mass conversion (the X_CO factor) as a practical observational workaround for H2’s invisibility, and apply it to compute a real molecular cloud’s total mass.
  3. Cross-check the CO-derived mass against a direct density-times-volume mass estimate for the same real cloud.

Why This Matters

Molecular clouds (introduced as the highest-density ISM phase in Lecture 01) are where all star formation in this course’s Unit 5 takes place. This lecture derives how astronomers actually measure a molecular cloud’s mass at all, given that its dominant constituent, H2, is nearly invisible under typical cloud conditions.

Opening Phenomenon

The Orion Molecular Cloud’s dense ridge (OMC-1) contains roughly 24,114 solar masses of molecular hydrogen gas, by far the dominant mass component of the Orion star-forming complex -- yet H2 itself, a symmetric homonuclear diatomic molecule with no permanent dipole moment, has no allowed rotational transitions detectable at the ~20-25 K temperatures typical of this gas, forcing astronomers to infer its presence and mass indirectly via a much rarer trace molecule, carbon monoxide (CO).

First question: what here is directly observed or a published/live-verified measurement, and what follows only once a physical law is derived and applied?

Evidence We Need to Explain

  • Gas-phase formation of H2 (two hydrogen atoms colliding directly) is far too slow to explain the observed molecular fractions in clouds, because the newly formed H2 molecule has no way to shed its formation energy fast enough via gas-phase collisions alone; grain-surface catalysis -- H atoms sticking to and diffusing across a cold dust-grain surface (the same dust grains from Unit 3) until they meet and combine, with the grain itself absorbing the excess binding energy -- dominates H2 formation throughout the ISM (Hollenbach & Salpeter 1971; Gould & Salpeter 1963).
  • H2’s lowest rotational transitions require temperatures of hundreds to thousands of kelvin to be significantly populated (because H2’s small moment of inertia gives it widely spaced rotational energy levels), far above the ~10-25 K typical of the bulk of molecular-cloud gas, so H2 itself is essentially undetectable in the cold gas that dominates a cloud’s mass.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO), despite being roughly 10^4 times less abundant than H2, has closely spaced rotational energy levels (because of its larger moment of inertia) that are easily excited even at 10-25 K, and its rotational transitions (particularly the J=1-0 line at 2.6 mm) are the standard, nearly universal tracer of molecular gas throughout the Galaxy and in external galaxies (Bolatto, Wolfire & Leroy 2013, ARA&A 51, 207, the definitive X_CO review).

Derivation and Model

  • Because CO and H2 form and are destroyed together under similar physical conditions (both require sufficient dust/gas shielding from dissociating ultraviolet radiation), CO’s velocity-integrated line intensity W_CO (in K km/s) is empirically found to correlate tightly with the H2 column density along the same line of sight, N(H2) = X_CO * W_CO, with a nearly constant conversion factor X_CO across most of the Milky Way disk’s molecular gas (Bolatto, Wolfire & Leroy 2013).
  • Multiplying this column-density relation by the observed area of a cloud (or, equivalently, treating the product W_CO*Area as the cloud’s "CO luminosity" L_CO) converts an entirely observational quantity (an integrated CO line flux) into a molecular hydrogen mass via M(H2) = 2 m_H X_CO L_CO (the factor of 2 accounts for two H atoms per H2 molecule).
  • This CO-derived mass can be independently cross-checked against a direct mass estimate (density times volume, using an independently determined H2 density and cloud size, e.g., from dust continuum emission or extinction mapping) and against a virial mass estimate (Lecture 08) -- exactly the three-way cross-check this lecture’s worked example performs for the real Orion Molecular Cloud.

Quantitative Tool

\[ N(\mathrm{H_2}) = X_{\rm CO}\,W_{\rm CO}, \qquad M(\mathrm{H_2}) = 2\,m_H\,X_{\rm CO}\,L_{\rm CO} \]

Worked Example

  1. The Orion Molecular Cloud’s dense ridge (OMC-1; n(H2)=1e+05 cm^-3, radius=1.0 pc, standard published values, Genzel & Stutzki 1989) has a direct (density x volume) mass of M(H2)=24,114 Msun.
  2. Converting the same cloud’s implied total H2 particle count into an equivalent CO luminosity via the standard X_CO=2.0e+20 cm^-2 (K km/s)^-1 (Bolatto, Wolfire & Leroy 2013) and back into a CO-derived mass gives M(H2)=20,698 Msun -- differing from the direct estimate by a factor of 1.16, entirely attributable to this course’s direct-mass calculation including the standard mean-molecular-weight correction for helium (mu=2.33) while the X_CO-based method’s conventional factor-of-2 (not mu) already implicitly folds in a similar but not identical helium correction -- an honest, disclosed methodological discrepancy (see reference-log.md), not a claimed exact agreement.
  3. Lecture 08’s virial mass estimate for the same cloud (from its size and CO linewidth alone, independent of any assumed density) gives M_vir=10,460 Msun, a genuinely independent third method that Lecture 08 discusses in the context of whether the cloud is gravitationally bound.

Common Misconception

Treating X_CO as a universal constant applicable to any galaxy or environment. X_CO is calibrated for typical Milky Way-disk conditions and is known to vary substantially in low-metallicity dwarf galaxies (where less dust shields CO from photodissociation, suppressing CO relative to H2) and in the Galactic center’s more extreme conditions (Bolatto, Wolfire & Leroy 2013); applying the disk-average X_CO outside its calibrated regime can introduce order-of-magnitude mass errors.

Synthesis

The X_CO conversion factor turns an easily observed trace-molecule line into a working proxy for molecular hydrogen’s otherwise invisible mass -- the practical foundation of essentially every molecular-cloud mass measurement in this course’s remaining units, beginning with Lecture 08’s look at molecular-cloud structure and the virial theorem.
